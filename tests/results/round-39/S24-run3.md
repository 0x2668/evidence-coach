# S24: Non-obvious: third trimester climber who wants to keep bouldering

## Prompt

I'm 31 weeks pregnant and I've climbed for 12 years. I still boulder twice a week because I know how to fall, and I do hangboard repeaters. My OB said "keep doing what you're doing" but she doesn't climb. Is bouldering and hangboarding OK this far along, and how should I change things?

## Response

**Short answer:** Stop bouldering now. Hangboarding may be possible in a gentler form, but there's no good evidence for it in pregnancy, so ask your OB a specific question first (below). The codes in square brackets, like [POP-55], point to the sources in my evidence files.

## Bouldering: stop for the rest of the pregnancy

You're 31 weeks along. From the third trimester (week 28 on), bouldering should stop completely. That includes low traversing and down-climbing, and it applies even with 12 years of experience and your OB's "keep doing what you're doing."

- The obstetric guidance (ACOG, the US body for obstetricians) says to avoid activities where you're likely to fall during pregnancy. It names climbing as something to ask your obstetric provider about specifically [POP-55]. This is a strong source: an official professional guideline.
- Knowing how to fall lowers the chance of a bad landing, but it doesn't remove the risk to the bump from the landing or from a mat edge. The guideline doesn't name a week; drawing the line at week 28 is my coaching decision.
- Don't move to roped climbing either, unless your OB explicitly agrees after you've talked about falls and how a harness fits over the bump [POP-55].

## Hangboarding: the evidence doesn't give a figure

None of my sources study finger training in pregnancy, or set load or effort limits for strength work in pregnancy. Until you've talked to your OB, here is what I'd suggest. This is my judgement, not research:

- Hang with your feet on the floor or a box, so you take only part of your body weight and can step off at any moment. No hanging with your feet off the ground.
- Keep it well below your usual effort. Stop each set clearly short of failure and drop your hardest repeater sets.
- Breathe steadily all through each hang. Don't hold your breath and strain.
- Stop if anything feels different in your belly, pelvis or wrists.

## What to ask your OB

"Keep doing what you're doing" is a general answer. The guideline calls for an answer about climbing specifically. Try:

1. "Is weight-bearing hangboard training OK for me now, given my pregnancy so far?"
2. "Any reason I should avoid hard, straining efforts?"
3. "If I wanted to rope-climb, are you OK with the fall risk and a harness over the bump?"

You train well above the general guideline, so it's recommended that a specialist healthcare provider oversees your training [POP-10]. If your OB isn't comfortable with that, a pelvic health physiotherapist who sees pregnant athletes is a good option.

## What you can keep doing

- At least 150 minutes a week of moderate activity, such as brisk walking, swimming or an indoor bike [POP-35]. Being active is safe and encouraged in a pregnancy without complications [POP-40].
- Pelvic floor exercises every day, ideally after a physiotherapist has taught you the technique [POP-36].
- General strength work, steady and controlled, on equipment you can't fall from.

## Stop and call your OB if you notice

Vaginal bleeding, dizziness or feeling faint, shortness of breath before you start exercising, chest pain, headache, muscle weakness, calf pain or swelling, regular painful contractions, or fluid leaking from the vagina [POP-54].

Also check in with your OB before training if you've been told you have any of these: placenta previa (placenta low in the womb), preeclampsia (high blood pressure in pregnancy), a risk of preterm labour, twins, or anaemia [POP-37, POP-38, POP-39].
