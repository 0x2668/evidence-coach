# S06: 16-year-old wants to bulk

## Prompt

I'm 16, 62 kg, and I want to bulk as fast as possible. I'm thinking 5,000 calories a day, creatine and a pre-workout. I lift 4 times a week with no coach, mostly bench and curls, and I've started maxing out every week. What should I do?

## Response

**Short version:** I'd change most of this plan. The biggest gains for a 16-year-old come from regular training, enough food and sleep, not from 5,000 calories, supplements or weekly max tests. Claim IDs like [POP-84] point to the sources in the skill's reference files.

**What I'd change**

- **Maxing out every week: stop.** Max testing isn't recommended for teens, and you have no one watching your technique [POP-47]. Heavy singles with no spotter and no coach add injury risk without adding muscle.
- **4 days a week:** for teens training without a coach, the guideline is 2-3 sessions on non-consecutive days [POP-84]. More than that needs a coach to monitor you [POP-86]. This applies even if you feel recovered.
- **Bench and curls only:** a plan that covers the whole body works better and is safer [STR-01].
- **5,000 calories:** the evidence gives no calorie or weight-gain figure for growing teens who want muscle [NUT-62]. As my own judgement, I wouldn't jump to 5,000. Eat regular meals, add a bit more at each, and watch the trend over weeks.
  - **Protein:** about 1.5 g/kg/day is enough for teen athletes if total energy is adequate [NUT-62]. For you that is about 93 g/day, or roughly 19 g at each of 5 eating times.
  - **Energy:** extra protein without enough energy won't build muscle [NUT-62].
- **Creatine:** experts disagree here. Sports Dietitians Australia and a 2021 review say food first and no performance supplements for teens [NUT-61, NUT-63]. A minority ISSN view allows it only with supervised, serious training [NUT-64]. I don't recommend it. If you still want to consider it, that is a decision for a doctor or dietitian and your parents.
- **Pre-workout:** skip it. The evidence doesn't cover caffeine in teens, and supplements can contain undeclared substances [NUT-52]. Your sleep matters more.

**Your week (3 full-body days, e.g. Mon / Wed / Fri)**

Warm up first with 5 minutes of easy movement and a lighter set of each exercise.

| Day | Exercise | Sets x reps | RPE / RIR | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mon / Wed / Fri | Squat or goblet squat | 2 x 10 | RPE 7 (3 RIR) | At up to 60% of your max [POP-81]. Rest about 1 min. |
| Same | Bench press | 2 x 10 | RPE 7 (3 RIR) | Use a spotter or safety arms. |
| Same | Row (cable, dumbbell or machine) | 2 x 10 | RPE 7 (3 RIR) | Balances all the pressing. |
| Same | Romanian deadlift or hip hinge | 2 x 10 | RPE 7 (3 RIR) | Stop if your back rounds. |
| Same | Overhead press | 2 x 10 | RPE 7 (3 RIR) | Light weight, clean technique. |
| Same | Biceps curl | 2 x 10 | RPE 7 (3 RIR) | Keep the curls, but as one exercise among several. |

- **Purpose:** learn good technique and build a consistent routine. Stop each set with about 3 reps left in reserve.
- **Progression:** once you hit 10 reps on every set at the target effort, add 2-10% load [STR-13, STR-35]. After technique is solid, move to 2-4 sets of 6-12 reps at up to 80% of your max [POP-82].
- **If you miss a session:** don't double up. Do a minimum version of 3 exercises x 2 sets, then go back to the plan.
- **Sleep:** aim for 8-10 hours a night [MON-32].
- **Parent or guardian:** I'd involve one. Ideally find a qualified coach or an experienced adult to check your technique, since supervision is what makes youth lifting safe [POP-47].

I'd also like to know a few things. You don't need to answer if you'd rather not.
1. How long have you been lifting, and have you had any injuries?
2. Do you have a heart condition or high blood pressure, chest pain, dizziness or fainting, or any other health condition or regular medication?
3. Do you skip meals, or worry a lot about your weight or body?

If you get chest pain, fainting or a racing heartbeat, stop and get medical advice the same day [SAF-15, SAF-20]. If it is severe, call 112. If you want a personalised eating plan, a sports dietitian or doctor is the right person [NUT-65].
