# 16-week plan: first 50 km trail ultra

This is doable, but tight. The goal is to finish by running the flats and gentle slopes and hiking the steep climbs. Your race could take roughly 8-10 hours, which is my rough estimate, so **check the race cut-off now**.

No controlled trials have tested ultra preparation. The long-run rule has good observational support, while the weekly km, climbing and back-to-back runs below are my judgement.

I've assumed the race is on the Saturday of week 16, and that you have access to hills and a gym.

## Week 1 in full

Zones use the 3-zone model, and run RPE uses the 6-20 scale. Your half marathon can't set pace zones because it's too long an effort, so use the talk test and RPE. Don't judge trail effort by pace.

| Day | Session / exercise | Sets x reps or time / zone | RPE / RIR | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mon | Squat (or goblet squat) | 2 x 8-10 | 3 RIR | Warm up with lighter sets |
| Mon | Calf raise | 2 x 8-10 | 3 RIR | Full range of motion |
| Mon, Thu | Row | 2 x 8-10 | 3 RIR | Do on both days |
| Mon, Thu | Push-up or dumbbell press | 2 x 8-10 | 3 RIR | Do on both days |
| Tue | Hills, 7 km | 15 min warm-up, then 6 x 2 min uphill in Z2 (steady, speech getting uncomfortable) | RPE 13-15 | Walk down between reps. On steep sections, hike. |
| Tue | Downhill practice (within the 7 km) | 5 min at about -10% grade, Z1 (easy) | RPE up to 11 | Short, relaxed steps. Some soreness is normal at first. |

**Purpose:** start trail, hill and strength work while keeping most of your running easy.

**If a session is missed:** don't stack it onto another day. Restart from the last level you completed. In a busy week, keep the long run if you can, plus one 30-minute easy run (my judgement).

**Rules for using the table**
- **Long run:** each one stays within 10% of your longest run in the previous 30 days. This is the best-supported progression rule, but no rule guarantees you won't get injured.
- **Time limit:** Saturday stops at 4 hours, even if you're short of the distance. From about week 9 you may hit this limit first, and that's fine.
- **Race distance:** the race itself is far longer than any training run. That's unavoidable in a first ultra with this time available.
- **Hills:** add 1 rep every week or two, up to 10 x 2 min (my judgement). In taper weeks, use fewer reps at the same effort.
- **Downhill:** build from 5 to 15 min by week 8 and keep some every week, because the protection can fade after about 9 weeks without it. Tuesday is the best day, since a hard downhill session can need up to 4-5 days before your next key run. Skip hard downhill in weeks 15-16.
- **Back-to-backs (weeks 12-13):** these are optional and based on coaching practice, not research. The source suggests 48-64 km over two days, but your schedule allows about 40-44 km. Make Sunday and Monday easy afterwards.
- **When to hold:** if effort rises for the same work, or sleep, soreness or fatigue get worse for several days, repeat the week instead of progressing.

**Strength**
- **Weeks 1-2:** do the sessions as written in week 1.
- **Weeks 3-13:** move to 3 x 6-8 at 2 RIR (RPE 8), working toward heavy loads of at least 80% of your 1-rep max. Add 2-10% load once you reach the top of the rep range at that effort.
- **Lighter weeks:** cut the sets rather than skipping the session.
- **From week 5:** add 3 x 6 low box jumps once a week, on Monday (dose is my judgement).
- **Taper:** do lighter sessions in weeks 14-15 and only one easy session in week 16 (my judgement).
- **No gym:** use loaded or single-leg versions of the same movements.

## Fuelling and drinking
Rehearse this on every run over an hour.

- **Carbohydrate:** build from 30 g/h towards 30-60 g/h, going higher only if your gut tolerates it. For the race, ISSN suggests 150-400 kcal/h, including 30-50 g carbohydrate and 5-10 g protein, with more savoury food as the hours pass. Experts disagree on the best carbohydrate rate, and 90 g/h is unlikely to be realistic for a race this long.
- **Fluid:** drink to thirst and take a planned sodium source. Experts disagree here too, but this is a sensible default until you know your sweat rate. Fluid is often around 450-750 mL/h in ultras, and the evidence gives no per-hour sodium dose.
- **Weight check:** weigh yourself before and after long runs. Aim to lose less than 2% of your body weight, and never gain weight, because that means you're over-drinking.
- **Daily food:** aim for 1.4-2.0 g protein per kg, and 6-10 g carbohydrate per kg on days with 1-3 hours of training.
- **Before the race:** you can carbohydrate-load at 10-12 g/kg/day for 36-48 hours, but try it before a long run first. Eat 1-4 g/kg of familiar carbohydrate 1-4 hours before the start.

## When to stop or get checked
- **Pain:** you can continue if it stays at 5/10 or below and is back to baseline by the next morning. If it goes above that, reduce the load. If it keeps happening or lasts a few weeks, see a physiotherapist or doctor.
- **Bone pain:** if you have pain in one specific spot on a bone, especially in the foot, stop progressing your running and get it checked. Stress fractures are more common in women who do ultras.
- **Periods:** if they become irregular or stop, see a doctor or sports dietitian. This can be a sign you're not eating enough for the training.
- **Today, before training again:** get medical advice for a racing or skipping heartbeat, fainting, or sudden unusual breathlessness.
- **Long runs and racing:**
  - Nausea, headache, dizziness, weakness or swollen hands can be early signs of low blood sodium. Stop, get medical help, and don't drink more water to treat it.
  - Confusion, vomiting, a seizure or collapse means call 112.

Repeated ultras may also carry long-term heart and kidney risks. That evidence is low quality, but you should know about it.

## Questions that could change the plan
1. What's the race date, cut-off and likely weather? A hot race would need a heat block of at least 60 min per day for 1-2 weeks.
2. Do you have hills nearby, or only a treadmill or stairs? Without a real descent, I'd need to change the downhill work.
3. Do you have a gym, and have you lifted before? If you're new to it, you'll stay on lighter loads for longer.
4. Why does this race matter to you, and what has got in the way of training before?
