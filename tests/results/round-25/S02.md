- **Long runs:** stop at 4 hours or the listed distance, whichever comes first. On hilly ground, 4 hours may only get you about 28-30 km, and that's fine.
- **The cap:** each run may be at most 10% longer than your longest run of the previous 30 days, because bigger jumps were linked to more overuse injuries [END-34]. If a run falls short, work out the next cap from what you actually ran.
- **Climbing:** aim for about 20 m per km in weeks 1-4, 30 in weeks 5-8 and 40 from week 9, which matches the race. These targets are my judgement, as the evidence gives no figure. If you have no hills, use an inclined treadmill or stairs.
- **Weekday limit:** Tuesday, Thursday and Friday runs stop at 2 hours.
- **Taper:** volume drops about 43% in week 15 and about 74% in week 16 before the race. That averages close to the recommended 41-60%, and you keep the same number of runs and some intensity [END-50].

## Strength

Strength work helps both running economy and injury prevention, and this is well supported [END-53, MON-39].

- **Weeks 1-4:** use the loads above, as I've assumed you're new to lifting [STR-15]. Log your reps and reps in reserve.
- **Weeks 5-13:** move the main leg lifts to about 6-8 reps at roughly 80% of your max, or a load that leaves 2-4 reps in reserve. Treat this as a first guess and correct it by feel [STR-03, STR-52].
- **Progression:** when you reach the top of the rep range on all sets at the target effort, add 2-10% [STR-35, STR-13].
- **Weeks 12 and 14:** halve the sets on Monday, which gives you easier days after the longest runs.
- **Weeks 15-16:** do one session a week with 1-2 sets, and do none in the 4 days before the race. This timing is my judgement.

## Race skills and fuelling

- **Effort:** nearly all your training and racing should be easy. Amateur runners in a 65 km mountain ultra spent about 86% of the race below their first threshold [END-40].
- **Climbs and pace:** hike the steep sections, and go by effort rather than pace, which means little on trails [END-46].
- **Eating on the run:** start with about 30 g of carbohydrate per hour on runs over 2 hours. Build toward 30-60 g per hour by week 9, using the foods you plan to race on [NUT-13, NUT-18, NUT-23]. Experts disagree on the upper limit for ultras.
- **Drinking:** drink to thirst and take a planned sodium source. Weigh yourself before and after long runs and aim to lose less than 2% [NUT-21, NUT-24, NUT-25]. Gaining weight during a run means you're drinking too much [NUT-31].
- **Daily food:** aim for 1.4-2.0 g of protein per kg of body weight. On the big training days, aim for about 6-10 g of carbohydrate per kg [NUT-01, NUT-12].

## What the evidence can't promise

- **Ultra training:** there are no controlled trials of ultra preparation, so the volumes and long-run lengths here are extrapolated and partly my judgement. No progression rule guarantees you won't get injured [END-30, END-34].
- **The race-day jump:** going from 32 km to 50 km far exceeds the 10% rule, and that's unavoidable in a first ultra on this schedule. Experienced ultrarunners also train more, averaging about 66-83 km a week, so walk early and often [END-37].
- **Back-to-back runs:** the longer Fridays in weeks 11 and 13 are a light version of this common but untested practice. Your time limits keep them under the suggested 48-64 km over two days, so skip them if your legs feel beaten up [END-45].
- **Long-term risk:** repeated ultras may carry heart and kidney risks, though this evidence is low quality [END-38].

## When to stop or get checked

- **Pain:** you can continue if pain stays at 5/10 or below and is back to baseline by the next morning; otherwise reduce the load [SAF-30]. See a physiotherapist or doctor if it hasn't improved within a few weeks [SAF-24].
- **Bone pain:** if you get localised bone pain, especially in the foot, stop the running progression and get it assessed. Stress fractures are more common in women who run ultras [END-39].
- **Periods or fatigue:** missed or irregular periods, or fatigue that lasts for weeks, are reasons to see a doctor or sports dietitian [SAF-61, SAF-64].
- **Emergencies:** call 112 for confusion, vomiting, a seizure or collapse during or after a long run or the race [SAF-49]. Chest pain or a racing, skipping heartbeat means stop training and get medical advice the same day [SAF-20].
