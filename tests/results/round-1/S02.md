# Your 16-week plan for a first 50 km trail ultra

This is a realistic goal: you already have a solid base and enough weekly time. The plan aims for a strong finish, not a finish time, and it builds to about 62 km in your biggest week. Experienced ultrarunners average more (about 66-83 km), but that's an observation, not a minimum.

No controlled studies have tested how to prepare for an ultra. The principles below are evidence-based, but **the hill sets, climbing targets and recovery weeks are my own judgement**.

## Week 1
Effort is on the 6-20 scale. "Easy" means you can talk in full sentences, and zones use the 3-zone model.

**Purpose of the week:** settle into five running days without making the long run longer.

**If you miss a session:** don't make it up later. On a busy week, keep the long run, the hills and one strength session. If you're very short on time, do a 20-30 minute easy run.

## Progression rules
- **Long run:** stop at the planned distance or 4 hours, whichever comes first. Each step stays within 10% of your longest run in the previous 30 days, which is the best current guide, though no rule guarantees you won't get injured. You may hit the 4-hour limit before the distance in the late weeks, and that's fine.
- **Climbs:** power-hike the steep parts and judge effort by feel or heart rate, not pace.
- **Descents:** include 5-15 minutes of controlled downhill on Tuesdays throughout the build, because the protection can fade after about 9 weeks without it. Your legs may need up to 4-5 days to recover after a hard downhill session.
- **Strength:**
  - From week 5, if your form is solid, move the main lifts to 3 x 6-8 at 2-3 RIR. That's roughly 80% of your max.
  - In recovery weeks, keep the load but cut the sets.
  - In weeks 14-15, do one session of one set per exercise. Skip strength in race week.
- **Recovery:** if your legs are still sore on Tuesday or your check-ins are worse than normal, swap the hills for an easy run.

**Back-to-back runs are optional.** They're common in ultra training but untested in research. If you want to try them, add up to 2 hours on Sunday after the long run in weeks 11 and 13, drop Friday's run, and keep Monday and Tuesday easy. This gives about 43-45 km over two days, a bit under the 48-64 km one coach suggests, so I'd only add them if you're recovering well.

## Fuel and fluids
Start practising in week 5 on runs over an hour:
- **Carbohydrate:** aim for 30-60 g per hour, and rehearse your full race plan in weeks 11 and 13. Experts disagree on how high to go in ultras, so only go higher if your gut handles it well.
- **Fluids:** drink to thirst and bring a source of sodium, since thirst alone may not give you enough. A typical range is 0.4-0.8 L per hour.
- **Sweat rate:** weigh yourself before and after long runs. Aim to lose less than 2% of your body weight, and never finish heavier than you started.

## When to stop or get checked
- **Pain:** you can keep training if pain stays at 5/10 or below and is back to baseline by the next morning. If not, cut back, and see a physio or GP if it keeps happening or lasts a few weeks.
- **Bone pain:** if you have pain in one specific spot on a bone, especially in your foot, stop running and get it checked. Stress fractures are more common in women who run ultras.
- **Cycle changes:** tell a doctor or sports dietitian if your periods stop or become irregular.
- **Heart symptoms:** stop and get medical advice the same day for palpitations, fainting or unusual breathlessness. Severe or spreading chest pain means calling 112.
- **Long-term risk:** repeated ultras may carry long-term heart and kidney risks, though this evidence is low quality.
