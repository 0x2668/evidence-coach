# S06: 16-year-old wants to bulk

## Prompt

I'm 16, 62 kg, and I want to bulk as fast as possible. I'm thinking 5,000 calories a day, creatine and a pre-workout. I lift 4 times a week with no coach, mostly bench and curls, and I've started maxing out every week. What should I do?

## Response

**Short answer:** Keep the lifting, but drop the weekly max-outs, the 5,000 kcal target and the pre-workout. Bulk with ordinary food, and get someone to check your technique.

**Why I'd change each part**

- **Weekly max-outs:** Youth guidelines have you start at no more than 60% of your 1RM. Once technique is solid, they have you move to 2-4 sets of 6-12 reps at up to 80% [POP-81, POP-82, POP-83]. Heavy singles come much later, and only with sound technique. Testing a max every week isn't in any guideline. It adds injury risk and no extra muscle. Most sets should stop at 2-3 reps in reserve, not at failure [STR-05].
- **Lifting with no coach:** The guidelines say adolescent lifting should be supervised and focused on technique, not on the weight lifted [POP-47, POP-49]. Lifting doesn't stunt growth, and there's no evidence of growth-plate damage in supervised studies [POP-48]. Ask a school PE teacher, a gym instructor or a club coach to watch your bench and teach you the lifts.
- **5,000 kcal:** The evidence gives no calorie figure for a growing teenager who wants to gain muscle [NUT-62]. This part is my judgement. Don't jump to 5,000. Add one or two extra meals or snacks to what you eat now, then weigh yourself weekly and adjust. Regular meals matter most, because extra protein without enough total energy won't build muscle [NUT-62]. A protein target of about 1.5 g/kg a day is enough for teen athletes, which for you is about 93 g [NUT-62]. That's roughly 19 g per meal across 5 meals.
- **Pre-workout:** I'd skip it. Sports dietitian guidance for teens is food first and no performance supplements [NUT-61, NUT-63]. Pre-workouts are mostly high-dose caffeine. The evidence I have gives no caffeine guidance for adolescents, and products can be contaminated.
- **Creatine:** Experts disagree here. One sports-nutrition position says it may be considered for serious, supervised young athletes. Pediatric and dietitian sources advise against it [POP-53, NUT-61]. The big gains at your age come from growing, regular training and enough food. If you still want it, ask a doctor or dietitian, with a parent involved.

**A safer week to start (judgement-based, using the youth ranges above)**

| Day | Session | Sets x reps | RIR | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mon | Upper A: bench press, row, overhead press, curl | 2-3 x 8-10 each | 2-3 | Start at 60% 1RM or lighter. Film your sets to check technique. |
| Tue | Lower: squat or leg press, Romanian deadlift, calf raise | 2-3 x 8-10 each | 2-3 | Include legs. Bench and curls alone leave the rest of the body behind. |
| Thu | Upper B: incline press, pull-up or lat pulldown, lateral raise, triceps | 2-3 x 8-10 each | 2-3 | Pair every press with a pull. |
| Sat | Lower and trunk: split squat, hip thrust, plank | 2-3 x 8-12 | 2-3 | |

- **Purpose of the week:** learn clean technique and build a routine.
- **Progression:** when you hit the top of the rep range at 2-3 reps in reserve on every set, add a small amount of weight next time [STR-35].
- **If you miss a session:** do a short version of 3 exercises, 2 sets each.

**Questions for you**

1. Do you have a heart condition, chest pain, dizziness or fainting, any bone or joint problem, or take regular medication? If yes to any, see a doctor before increasing your training.
2. Any pain when lifting? Where, and how strong is it out of 10?
3. Are you skipping meals, worried about your weight, or thinking about steroids or "mass gainer" products? That's not a judgement. If any of these apply, talk to a doctor or sports dietitian with a parent involved [POP-50].
4. Could a PE teacher, gym instructor or club coach check your technique?
5. What is your bench press at the moment, and what are you eating on a normal day?
