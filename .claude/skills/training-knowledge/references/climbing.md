# Climbing: finger training and injuries

Evidence levels: consensus, meta-analysis, single study, expert opinion. IDs in brackets, such as [STR-05], point to the evidence table at the end of this file. Generated from the verified research notes on 2026-10-30; do not edit by hand.

## Practical rules

### What to train
- Treat finger flexor strength, forearm endurance and upper-body power as the main trainable physical qualities for climbing. Do not promise that balance, flexibility or a lower body weight will improve performance. [CLB-01, CLB-57]
- For intermediate and advanced adult climbers, add structured finger or upper-body resistance training to climbing. It improves climbing-specific test results more than climbing alone (moderate effect): finger strength, rate of force development and forearm endurance all improve. Tell the athlete that these are test scores, not proven grade gains. A 10-week hangboard trial (two sessions per week, intermediate to advanced climbers) raised maximal finger strength more than climbing alone [CLB-02, CLB-03, CLB-63, CLB-64]. A systematic review of controlled studies suggests mixing maximal-strength with hypertrophy or endurance work, semispecific exercises, and both static and dynamic exercises, but these are trends [CLB-61, CLB-62].
- Present the hangboard protocols below as reasonable options, not as the proven best. The evidence cannot rank specific protocols. [CLB-04] In a 4-week trial of experienced climbers on a 12 mm hold, 100% and 80% of maximal finger strength improved maximal strength, 80% and 60% improved stamina and endurance, so the intensity can be matched to the goal [CLB-65, CLB-66].

### Who should hangboard, and when (readiness gates)
- Complete beginners (roughly the first year): build finger strength by climbing and spend the time on skill, not on hangboarding. This rule comes from practitioner guidance, not trials. [CLB-22]
- A survey of Finnish climbers found regular fingerboard training linked to less finger pain in climbers with 6 or more years of climbing but to more finger pain in male climbers with under 6 years who had reached 7a or higher; this cross-sectional finding supports the experience gate below [CLB-71].
- Before prescribing weighted or maximal hangs, check all of these (practitioner criteria, low certainty): about 2 years of regular climbing at 2-3 days per week; can hang 15 s on a 25 mm edge; injury-free; age 16 or older and past the growth spurt. If the athlete fails the edge test, keep building finger strength through climbing (steeper walls, some small holds). [CLB-23, CLB-24, CLB-25]
- For climbers who do not yet meet the gate but want finger work, use only low-intensity, feet-on-the-floor hangs (18-22 mm edge, a light strain in the forearms; practitioner example 20 s hangs with a foot on the floor). Label the evidence as weak: one non-randomised app study. [CLB-11, CLB-12, CLB-21]

### Dose and progression of hangboard training (adults who pass the gate)
- Frequency: 2 sessions per week with 48-72 h between them for improvement, which matches the trials. One session per week maintains gains. Put max hangs and campus work at the start of the session, when fresh. [CLB-07, CLB-13, CLB-26, CLB-35, CLB-60]
- Max hangs (strength): 10 s hangs at a load that would allow about 13 s (stop about 3 s short of failure), 3 to 5 sets, 3 min rest, in the half-crimp (the trials' grip, considered safer than the full crimp). [CLB-05, CLB-59]
- Intermittent hangs (strength-endurance, a repeater variant): sets of 4 ten-second hangs with 5 s rest between hangs, 3 to 5 sets, 1 min between sets, on an edge where the last repetition of the last set is near failure. Intermittent hangs gave the largest grip-endurance gain in one small trial (45% compared with 34% for max hangs), but the difference was not significant. [CLB-06, CLB-08]
- Starting and progressing: start any new method with 2 sets and build 2-3-4-4 sets over 4 weeks. Then take 1-2 weeks without hangs before the next cycle. Increase load in small steps: add 1.25 kg only after the target 10 s hang is completed in the last set. One trial added about 1.25 kg per week over 8 weeks in hangboard-naive intermediate climbers and found a small strength gain. [CLB-09, CLB-10, CLB-27]
- Hold each phase of finger loading for months, not weeks, and progress finger load more slowly than muscle strength seems to allow. Tendons adapt to heavy loading (above about 70% of maximal effort), but more slowly than muscle, and the clearest tendon effects appear after more than 12 weeks (data from leg tendons, not fingers). [CLB-16, CLB-17, CLB-18, CLB-19, CLB-20]
- Campus board: only for advanced, experienced adults, with the fingers warmed up, at the start of the session, never when fatigued. A five-week block improved finger strength and bouldering in advanced-to-elite climbers; two weekly sessions improved bouldering (effect size 2.59) and did not differ significantly from four. For lower-grade climbers, limit explosive finger work and campusing. [CLB-14, CLB-15, CLB-35, CLB-36, CLB-67, CLB-68]
- Use the half-crimp rather than the full crimp for hangboard training: in the crimp grip the A2 pulley carried about 3 times the force applied at the fingertip (up to 116N measured), and bowstringing was smaller in the slope grip [CLB-69, CLB-70]. Crimp-grip use, higher climbing intensity, bouldering, low finger strength and previous injury are associated with overuse injury. Ask about these when an athlete's load rises. [CLB-41, CLB-59]

### Youth climbers (under 18)
- No feet-off or dynamic campus boarding under 18. Treat this as the agent's default even though some coaches disagree. [CLB-34, CLB-36, CLB-39]
- Keep out intensive finger strength and power training (weighted or maximal hangs, campusing) for adolescents. Reduce finger training intensity during the growth spurt (about 13-15 years). Favour flexibility, coordination and technique. Limit crimping. [CLB-25, CLB-29, CLB-32, CLB-33, CLB-34, CLB-37]
- Ask the athlete or parent to track height and weight about every three months. Lower training intensity during fast growth (outgrowing clothes or shoes). [CLB-38]
- Monitor load and recovery, and explain to young athletes and parents that growth-plate stress injuries are the most common injury in injured adolescent climbers. They appear mostly in boys around 13-15, in the middle and ring fingers, without any single traumatic event. In a 2026 scoping review, 98% of 149 physeal stress injuries were at the middle finger joint, mean age 14 years; 91% of the adolescents returned to climbing (2 weeks to 6 months) [CLB-28, CLB-30, CLB-31, CLB-32, CLB-33, CLB-72, CLB-73].

### Shoulders, elbows and general strength
- Include general and shoulder strength work (closed-chain, eccentric and general strength exercises; rotator cuff and forearm extensors) alongside climbing. The only climbing prevention trial (one cohort, 84 climbers) reduced shoulder and elbow injuries with this kind of work 3-4 times per week for a year; fewer sessions are an untested compromise. Say that no programme has been shown to prevent finger injuries. [CLB-46, CLB-47, CLB-48]
- Fingers, shoulders and elbows are the most common chronic injury sites. Check on them in feedback questions. [CLB-30, CLB-40]

### Warm-up
- Prescribe a warm-up for performance and readiness: general mobilisation of neck, shoulders, arms, wrists and fingers, then 3-5 progressive hangs on a 15-20 mm edge (10 to 20 s) before hard finger work. Ten-minute warm-ups (dynamic stretching, vibration rolling or a climbing-specific routine) improve shoulder mobility but did not raise finger strength, so finger-specific ramping is needed before maximal finger efforts. Do not claim that the warm-up is proven to prevent injury. [CLB-49, CLB-50, CLB-51, CLB-52]

### Body weight and eating
- Do not set weight-loss goals to improve climbing. Redirect to sport-specific strength and endurance. No study shows that losing weight makes a climber better. [CLB-57, CLB-58]
- Screen all climbers, and especially female and competitive climbers, for signs of disordered eating and menstrual disturbance. Use the SAF/NUT referral rules when a sign appears. [CLB-53, CLB-54, CLB-55, CLB-56]

## Red flags and referral triggers

The agent gives no diagnosis or treatment for any of these. It stops or modifies the relevant loading and refers.
- Sudden finger loading with a heard or felt pop, pain over the affected pulley, swelling afterwards, or visible bowstringing of the flexor tendons: stop climbing and hangboarding on that hand and refer to a physician or hand/sports specialist experienced with climbing injuries. Bowstringing suggests multiple ruptured pulleys. [CLB-42, CLB-43]
- Any persistent or chronic finger pain in an adolescent climber, especially at the middle finger joint (the stress fractures sit at the dorsal base of the middle phalanx): refer promptly for medical evaluation with imaging. Remove intensive finger training until cleared. [CLB-28, CLB-29, CLB-31]
- Overuse pain that is spreading from after climbing to the start of the session, during climbing, or all the time: refer; do not wait until pain is constant. [CLB-44]
- Shoulder pain that persists or recurs: refer for medical diagnosis. [CLB-45]
- A young athlete's height or weight drops two percentile lines on a growth chart: refer to a doctor. [CLB-38]
- Disordered eating signs, missed periods (amenorrhea) or rapid weight loss: refer under the SAF/NUT rules. [CLB-53, CLB-55, CLB-56]

## Contested areas

### Which hangboard protocol is best
- Position A: the trials support adding finger training, but they are too few and too similar to rank protocols. [CLB-04]
- Position B: practitioners prescribe specific systems (max hangs vs. intermittent hangs, set progressions, edge sizes) with more confidence. [CLB-26, CLB-27]
- Position C: a 4-week trial shows that the intensity of intermittent and maximal hangs steers which quality improves, so protocol choice should follow the goal [CLB-66].
- Why they differ: the trials are short, small, unblinded and in advanced men; practitioner systems come from coaching experience.
- What the agent should do: pick a protocol to match the goal (max hangs for strength, intermittent hangs for strength-endurance), dose it as above, and judge it by the athlete's response over a 4-week cycle, as in the practitioner progression [CLB-27, CLB-04]. Tell the athlete: "Both styles work in studies; we pick the one that fits your goal and adjust from how you respond."

### Low-intensity daily hangs ("Abrahangs")
- Position A: frequent feet-on low-intensity hangs gave finger strength gains similar to max hangs and added to them. [CLB-11, CLB-12]
- Position B: the data are retrospective, self-logged and non-randomised, and the protocol's designer co-authored the study. [CLB-11]
- What the agent should do: offer them as a low-risk option, for example for climbers below the max-hang gate, but do not present them as a proven substitute for heavier training.

### How long fingers need to adapt
- Position A: tendons may need at least 2 years, or about 5 times longer than muscle; this is expert and practitioner estimate, without data. [CLB-20, CLB-21, CLB-23]
- Position B: leg-tendon stiffness changes measurably within 8-12 weeks of heavy loading, although more slowly than muscle. [CLB-16, CLB-17, CLB-18, CLB-19]
- What the agent should do: progress finger load slowly and conservatively (small load steps, deload weeks, readiness gates) because finger-specific data are missing, but do not tell athletes they must wait years before any finger training. Explain it this way: "Tendons do adapt, but more slowly than muscle, so we build up finger load gradually."

### Age and experience limits for campus and hangboard training
- Positions: under 18, no feet-off or dynamic campusing (BMC/UIAA MedCom) [CLB-34]; campusing only after 8-10 years of climbing and not until 16-17 (UIAA factsheet) [CLB-36]; intensive finger training from about 16 and after the growth spurt (practitioner) [CLB-25]. Most surveyed US youth coaches use feet-off campusing with some adolescents. [CLB-39]
- A Finnish survey found regular fingerboard training linked to less finger pain after 6 or more years of climbing but to more finger pain in male climbers with under 6 years who had reached 7a or higher; cross-sectional, so no cause can be shown [CLB-71].
- Why they differ: there are no trials; the thresholds rest on case series of growth-plate stress fractures and on expert judgement.
- What the agent should do: apply the strictest common rule. Under 18: no feet-off or dynamic campusing, and no weighted or maximal hangs. Adults: campus only after several years of structured training and advanced level. If a human coach decides otherwise for an adolescent, note the disagreement and the injury signs to watch for. [CLB-34, CLB-35, CLB-36, CLB-39]

### Warm-up and injury prevention
- Position A: a proper warm-up including the fingers helps prevent injury (UIAA MedCom; BMC). [CLB-35, CLB-52]
- Position B: observational studies find no protective association of warm-up or cool-down with overuse injury. [CLB-51]
- What the agent should do: keep a finger-specific warm-up because it prepares the athlete for hard efforts and costs little. Tell the athlete it is good preparation but not proven injury protection.

### Antagonist and "imbalance" training
- Position A: overuse injuries come from muscle imbalances, so train the rotator cuff and forearm extensors (expert opinion). [CLB-48]
- Position B: the only trial evidence covers shoulder and elbow injuries in one small cohort; the imbalance mechanism has not been tested. [CLB-46, CLB-47]
- What the agent should do: include shoulder, pushing and extensor work as low-cost general strength training, without claiming it prevents finger injuries.

### Are climbers at higher risk of disordered eating?
- Position A: screening-questionnaire disordered eating appears in 8.6% of international lead climbers and 16.5% of the women among them. [CLB-53]
- Position B: advanced and elite Swedish climbers did not differ from controls, though women had far more symptoms than men (26% vs. 5.8%). [CLB-54]
- What the agent should do: do not label climbers as a high-risk group overall. Screen every climber, with particular attention to female and competitive climbers, and never reinforce weight-loss goals.

## Evidence gaps

- No trials in beginners, women only, or adolescents; no long-term training trials; no trial with climbing grade as the main outcome.
- No prospective trial of any finger-injury prevention programme.
- No finger-tendon or pulley adaptation time course in humans under controlled loading; progression advice borrows from leg-tendon data.
- Youth age thresholds for hangboard and campus training rest on expert opinion and case series.
- Disordered-eating prevalence data are cross-sectional, self-selected and use different questionnaires; REDs details belong to SAF and NUT.
