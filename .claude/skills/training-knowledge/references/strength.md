# Strength, hypertrophy, periodization, mobility

Evidence levels: consensus, meta-analysis, single study, expert opinion. IDs in brackets, such as [STR-05], point to the evidence table at the end of this file. Generated from the verified research notes on 2026-10-30; do not edit by hand.

## Practical rules

### Weekly structure and minimum dose
- Default for any healthy adult: resistance training at least 2 sessions per week, high effort, all major muscle groups [STR-01, STR-03]. Cover at least upper-body push, upper-body pull, lower-body push (squat or leg press pattern) and lower-body pull (hinge) every week; splitting upper body into horizontal and vertical is optional [STR-11, STR-23].
- Minimum effective plan (for an adult with only a few short sessions per week): 1 squat or leg-press pattern, 1 upper pull and 1 upper push per session, at least 4 weekly sets per muscle group at 6-15 RM, ending sets near failure [STR-23, STR-19]. In trained people, even 1 set of 6-12 reps at about 70-85% 1RM, 2-3 times per week, still raises strength, just less than more volume does [STR-22]. Tell the athlete honestly that this is enough to progress but not the maximum [STR-16, STR-20].
- For a previously untrained adult who can only manage one session in some weeks, a single weekly resistance session and single-set training are the best-supported minimal-dose strategies; they raise strength more than no resistance training, but they are a floor, not a target [STR-68, STR-69].
- Maintenance during a busy or interrupted period: in younger people, strength and muscle size were maintained for up to 32 weeks with 1 session per week and 1 set per exercise if the relative load stayed the same; in older people plan up to 2 sessions per week and 2-3 sets per exercise. Keep the load heavy and cut volume and frequency instead [STR-55, STR-56].
- When sessions are short, use supersets, drop sets or rest-pause sets to cut session time roughly in half; prefer these for hypertrophy work, and keep straight sets with full rest for the main strength lifts [STR-24].
- Frequency is a scheduling tool: at equal weekly volume, 1 versus more sessions per muscle per week makes no meaningful difference to hypertrophy, so pick the split that fits the athlete's week [STR-26, STR-17, STR-06]. For maximal strength in a specific lift, practise it at least 2 times per week; extra frequency helps strength with diminishing returns [STR-03, STR-17].

### Volume
- Count weekly sets per muscle group. Count a set that trains the muscle indirectly (triceps in a bench press, glutes in a squat) as half a set [STR-18].
- Hypertrophy: start beginners and time-limited athletes around 4-10 weekly sets per muscle; a standard target is at least 10 weekly sets per muscle [STR-19, STR-02, STR-20]. Above roughly 18-20 weekly sets, each further gain needs many more sets (about 10 or more extra sets per detectable gain), so add volume only for advanced athletes who are recovering well and have stalled [STR-04, STR-20]. Avoid going above about 25 weekly sets per muscle: few studies have tested that range [STR-19].
- Strength: 2-3 sets per exercise per session and only a few hard weekly sets per lift capture most of the gain; returns flatten much faster than for hypertrophy, so raise intensity and practice frequency before adding many sets [STR-03, STR-04, STR-16, STR-21].
- Add volume in small steps and only when recovery and performance are good; the evidence says more volume helps with diminishing returns but does not define a step size [STR-16, STR-20].

### Load and repetition ranges
- Hypertrophy: any load from about 30% to 100% of 1RM works if sets end close to failure; 6-15 RM is the time-efficient middle; lighter loads (more than 15 RM) need sets taken closer to failure [STR-06, STR-25, STR-23].
- Strength: include heavy work at 80% of 1RM or more (for trained lifters, emphasis on 1-6 RM loads), full range of motion, with the main lift early in the session [STR-03, STR-25, STR-14]. A network meta-analysis agrees: loads above 80% of 1RM gave the largest strength gains, whereas hypertrophy was similar across the loads, sets and frequencies compared (highest-ranked: higher-load, multiple sets, 3 sessions per week for strength; twice per week for hypertrophy, with many similar options) [STR-66, STR-67].
- Beginners (no training, or none for several years): start with 8-12 RM loads; treat an athlete as intermediate after about 6 months of consistent training [STR-15].
- Converting %1RM to repetitions: on average about 5 reps at 90% and about 15 at 70% of 1RM, with wide individual spread (SD about 2.5 reps at 80%, about 4.4 reps at 60%); more reps are possible on the leg press. Use such tables, or 1RM estimates from repetitions, only as a first guess and correct with RIR [STR-52].
- Tempo, machines versus free weights, and time of day can be chosen freely: they did not change strength or hypertrophy outcomes [STR-06, STR-07].

### Effort: RIR and RPE
- In plan tables use the RIR-based RPE scale: RPE 10 = 0 RIR, RPE 9 = 1 RIR, RPE 8 = 2 RIR, and so on [STR-29].
- Default working-set target: about 2-3 RIR, which is RPE 8 at 2 RIR on the scale above [STR-05, STR-28, STR-29]. Training to failure is not required for strength or hypertrophy [STR-05, STR-28].
- Main compound lifts (squat, bench, deadlift, overhead press): mostly 2-4 RIR (RPE 6-8) [STR-31]. Use 0 RIR (failure) only on the last set for a body part, and only on simple, low-risk machine or isolation exercises, when hypertrophy is the goal [STR-31, STR-27].
- For strength goals, stopping further from failure costs little [STR-27]; for hypertrophy, sets that end closer to failure are probably somewhat better (see Contested areas) [STR-27, STR-28].
- Beginners: have them log RIR from the start, but base load and progression on repetitions and load until their RIR ratings prove accurate; a check is to rate a set and then take the same load to failure on a safe exercise (machine or with a spotter) [STR-30].
- Assume reported RIR is off by about 1 repetition (people usually underestimate what they have left); ratings are more accurate near failure, with heavier loads (12 or fewer reps) and in later sets [STR-32].

### Rest between sets
- Hypertrophy: rest at least 60-90 s; longer is fine and not worse [STR-33].
- Strength in trained lifters: rest more than 2 min on main lifts (3-5 min for heavy 1-6 RM sets is the traditional default) [STR-34, STR-14]. Untrained people: 60-120 s is enough [STR-34, STR-07].
- If time is short, shorten rest on accessory exercises first, or use supersets, rather than cutting rest on heavy main lifts [STR-24, STR-34].

### Progression
- Use double progression as the default: keep the load until the athlete reaches the top of the rep range on all sets at the target RIR, then add load and drop back to the bottom of the range. Rep progression and load progression gave similar results [STR-35].
- Load step: increase by 2-10% once the athlete can do 1-2 reps more than the target with the current load; use the smaller step for upper-body and small-muscle exercises [STR-13].
- Progression is needed for continued long-term gains, but it is not needed to get benefits; for a beginner or someone returning, consistency comes first [STR-09]. In a trial of untrained young women, adding load when the top of the rep range was reached roughly doubled triceps growth (about 22.9% versus 11.6% with a fixed load over 8 weeks), yet the fixed-load arm still grew [STR-59, STR-60].
- If performance drops or reported RIR is clearly lower than planned for the same load, hold or reduce the load before adding more [STR-30, STR-32]. (Rule of thumb from the RIR literature; the trigger thresholds are not tested.)

### Deloads
- A deload is a planned or autoregulated period of reduced training stress to reduce fatigue and prepare for the next block [STR-36].
- How: keep training days and exercises, cut sets per session and/or reps per set, and reduce load or effort (more RIR) [STR-37, STR-38]. Prefer a reduced-volume week over a full week off when the athlete's goal is strength: a complete 1-week break mid-program reduced strength gains in one trial [STR-39].
- When: competitive strength and physique athletes typically deload about 6 days every 5-6 weeks, pre-planned and/or autoregulated [STR-38, STR-53]. Use that as the most frequent schedule for advanced athletes in hard blocks; for beginners and recreational athletes, deload when the data say so (performance stalls, muscle soreness or joint aches increase) rather than on a fixed calendar [STR-37, STR-53]. A recent trial in untrained young men found that planned deload weeks (weeks 4 and 8 of 8, once per week for 2 sets per exercise) did not hinder muscle thickness or strength-endurance gains, so a deload is safe to include but not shown to help [STR-57, STR-58]. The evidence that deloads improve outcomes is weak (see Contested areas).

### Periodization and autoregulation
- Any well-progressed program works for general fitness and hypertrophy; do not oversell periodization [STR-08, STR-40].
- For trained athletes chasing maximal strength, vary load and volume across days or weeks (undulating), which gave a small 1RM advantage over linear models in trained people [STR-40, STR-41]. An older meta-analysis found a larger periodization advantage (effect size 0.43) that is not volume-controlled; treat it as the optimistic end [STR-72].
- RIR-based autoregulation and fixed percentages give similar strength gains; use RIR (or bar velocity if the athlete has a device) to adjust daily loads, especially when sleep, stress or endurance training vary [STR-42, STR-05]. Velocity-based and percentage-based loading gave no significant difference in one small meta-analysis and a small edge (effect size 0.26) to velocity-based loading in another; do not buy equipment for it [STR-70, STR-71].

### Concurrent training (runners, cyclists, climbers who lift)
- Endurance training does not meaningfully blunt hypertrophy or maximal strength in most people; explosive strength is more sensitive [STR-43]. Men may lose a little lower-body strength gain [STR-44]. An umbrella review found that adding endurance work to resistance training improved aerobic capacity (SMD 0.77) while strength, power and hypertrophy stayed comparable; the order (lifting first versus endurance first) showed no significant effect, only a trend favouring lifting first [STR-61, STR-62].
- Schedule lifting and hard endurance sessions at least 3 h apart, ideally on different days, especially when explosive strength matters [STR-43]. Detailed strength programming for endurance performance belongs to END.

### Bodyweight and minimal equipment
- Elastic bands, home-based and bodyweight training improve strength (bands also hypertrophy); push-ups loaded to a similar relative load matched bench-press gains in one small study [STR-10, STR-45].
- Make bodyweight work hard enough: progress the variation (incline to floor to deficit or weighted), slow the tempo or add reps until sets end near failure, since light loads only work near failure [STR-25, STR-45]. Bodyweight-only training did not improve range of motion, so add load or end-range work if mobility is a goal [STR-48].

### Warm-up, mobility and flexibility
- Warm up before training; warm-ups other than stretching alone improved performance in most outcomes studied [STR-50]. When time is short, exercise-specific warm-up sets are enough, and stretching is only needed when flexibility is a goal [STR-54]. An expert panel describes the warm-up as general exercise, then specific exercise, then optional conditioning activities (heavy voluntary contractions); it may lower long-term injury risk but gives no acute injury protection, so do not promise injury prevention from a warm-up [STR-74, STR-73].
- Keep static stretching before strength or power work under 60 s per muscle group, and follow it with dynamic activity; 60 s or more reduced performance by about 4.6% [STR-46, STR-47].
- Range of motion: full-range resistance training improves it about as much as stretching [STR-48]. If the athlete wants more flexibility, add static stretching of about 4 min per session and about 10 min per week; more gives no extra benefit [STR-49]. Acute stretch gains fade within about 30 min [STR-47].

### Knee pain during squats (programming only; SAF owns red flags and referral)
- First apply SAF's red-flag and referral rules; if any red flag is present, stop the exercise and follow SAF.
- Without red flags, keep training the area and modify the stimulus: reduce load and move to the lighter end of the rep range (hypertrophy is similar with lighter loads taken near failure) [STR-25, STR-06]; slow the tempo, which does not reduce hypertrophy [STR-06]; change body position or exercise variation (box squat, leg press, split squat, hinge-dominant work) [STR-12]; or temporarily shorten the range of motion to a comfortable depth [STR-51]. If depth is limited, keep working the stretched (lengthened) part of the movement where it is comfortable, since lengthened partial repetitions matched full range in trained adults' upper-body training (not tested for squats) [STR-64, STR-63, STR-65].
- Explain the trade-off: partial range of motion gives somewhat less strength and lower-limb hypertrophy than full range on average, but it depends on which part of the range is trained (see Contested areas); restore depth progressively as symptoms allow [STR-51, STR-65].
- Log pain with each session; if it persists or worsens despite modification, hand over to SAF referral rules [STR-12].

### Youth (for example a 16-year-old)
- Apply POP's youth rules for supervision, loading and technique; use the programming principles here only within those limits.

## Red flags and referral triggers

Not owned by STR. Use SAF for pain red flags and referral. STR's only rule: when any exercise causes pain, modify load, range, tempo or variation first, and refer per SAF if pain is severe, persistent or worsening [STR-12].

## Contested areas

### Upper limit of useful weekly volume for hypertrophy
- Position A: returns diminish and roughly plateau beyond about 18-20 weekly sets per muscle (ACSM, citing a meta-regression) [STR-04].
- Position B: no clear plateau; each additional detectable gain simply needs more sets, with uncertainty above about 25 weekly sets [STR-19, STR-20].
- Why they differ: different set-counting methods (direct versus fractional sets), few studies at very high volumes, short studies that do not capture fatigue or burnout [STR-19, STR-20].
- What the agent should do: program roughly 10 to 20 weekly sets per muscle for most people (at least 10 for hypertrophy, with diminishing returns from about 18-20) [STR-02, STR-04]; go higher only for advanced athletes with good recovery, temporarily, and back off if performance or motivation drops. The upper end of the range is a judgement between the two positions, not a measured optimum [STR-04, STR-19, STR-20].
- How to explain: "More sets help, but each extra set is worth less than the one before; beyond roughly 18-20 per muscle the extra benefit is small and uncertain." [STR-04, STR-20]

### How close to failure
- Position A: failure is not required; 2-3 RIR is sufficient for strength, hypertrophy and power [STR-05, STR-28].
- Position B: hypertrophy keeps improving as sets end closer to failure; strength does not [STR-27].
- Why they differ: categorical comparisons (failure versus non-failure) versus continuous dose-response models with RIR estimated from study descriptions; RIR is hard to estimate accurately [STR-32].
- What the agent should do: about 2-3 RIR as the default [STR-05]; for hypertrophy, 0 RIR only on the last set of a simple isolation exercise, and mostly 2-4 RIR on heavy compound lifts [STR-31]; for strength goals, stopping further from failure costs little [STR-27].
- How to explain: "Getting close to failure matters; actually reaching it rarely adds much and costs more fatigue."

### Rest intervals for strength
- Position A: short (under 1 min) versus long (over 1 min) rest did not change strength [STR-07].
- Position B: trained lifters need more than 2 min to maximise strength; the 2009 ACSM stand advised 3-5 min for heavy sets [STR-34, STR-14].
- Why they differ: the ACSM 2026 evidence base is mostly novices; the trained-lifter data come from fewer, older studies.
- What the agent should do: more than 2 min on heavy main lifts for trained athletes (3-5 min for heavy 1-6 RM sets is the traditional default); 60-120 s for beginners; 1-2 min for accessory hypertrophy work [STR-34, STR-14, STR-07].

### Periodization
- Position A: with progressive overload, periodized programs are not significantly better [STR-08].
- Position B: periodized and undulating programs give a small 1RM advantage, mainly in trained people [STR-40, STR-41]; an older, not volume-matched meta-analysis found a moderate advantage (effect size 0.43) [STR-72].
- What the agent should do: keep beginners on simple progression; use undulating loading for trained athletes who want maximal strength, and plan peaking blocks for competitions (expert practice, little trial evidence).

### Deloads
- Position A: coaches agree deloads reduce fatigue and are widely used every 5-6 weeks [STR-36, STR-37, STR-38].
- Position B: the only trial of a full week off mid-program found lower strength gains and no hypertrophy benefit [STR-39]; a reduced-volume deload in untrained men neither helped nor hurt [STR-57].
- Why they differ: practice is built on coaching experience; trials are few, short and test complete cessation rather than reduced training.
- What the agent should do: use reduced-volume deloads (fewer sets, more RIR) when fatigue indicators accumulate, or pre-plan them at roughly the surveyed interval (mean 5.6 weeks) for advanced athletes in hard blocks [STR-38, STR-53]; never sell deloads as proven to boost gains.
- How to explain: "A lighter week is a tool to manage fatigue; the science on it is thin, so we use it when your numbers and how you feel say you need it."

### Range of motion: full versus partial
- Position A: full range of motion gave greater strength and lower-limb hypertrophy than partial range [STR-51].
- Position B: partial range in the lengthened (initial) part of the movement matched full range for hypertrophy in a systematic review and in a trial of trained adults' upper-body training; partial range in the final (shortened) part did worse for several muscles [STR-65, STR-63, STR-64].
- Why they differ: "partial" covers different parts of the range, muscles respond differently, and the trials are short and mostly upper body.
- What the agent should do: default to full range for strength and hypertrophy; for knee-pain or mobility limits, partial range in the stretched part of the movement is a reasonable temporary option, not a permanent replacement, and stay away from only training the shortened end of the range [STR-51, STR-65, STR-64].
- How to explain: "Full range is the safe default; if we need to shorten the movement we shorten it where the muscle is stretched, not at the top."

### Velocity-based versus percentage-based loading
- Position A: no difference in strength outcomes between velocity-based and percentage-based training [STR-70].
- Position B: small advantage for velocity-based training, mainly in athletes [STR-71].
- What the agent should do: both work; use RIR or velocity (if the athlete owns a device) to adjust loads, and choose the method the athlete will actually use [STR-42, STR-70, STR-71].

### Concurrent training interference
- Position A: no meaningful interference for maximal strength or hypertrophy [STR-43].
- Position B: small lower-body strength interference in men [STR-44].
- Position C: an umbrella review found comparable strength and hypertrophy for combined versus resistance-only training and no significant effect of order; trends favoured lifting before endurance work [STR-61, STR-62].
- What the agent should do: separate hard endurance and lower-body strength sessions by at least 3 h or by days [STR-43]; tell male endurance athletes that lower-body strength may rise a little more slowly [STR-44].

## Evidence gaps

- No trial has compared deload frequencies or designs; all deload timing advice is expert opinion or survey data.
- Evidence on volume, frequency and failure comes mostly from young men in studies of about 8-12 weeks; women, older adults and highly trained athletes are underrepresented.
- No good evidence on how many weeks of a given volume a person can sustain before fatigue outweighs gains.
- Programming modifications for knee pain (depth, tempo, exercise choice) have no direct trials in otherwise healthy lifters; recommendations rely on hypertrophy evidence for lighter loads, tempo and partial range, plus one observation from clinical exercise trials.
- Accuracy and usefulness of RIR in beginners, and the right progression trigger based on RIR, are untested.
- Maintenance-dose evidence (Spiering 2021) is a narrative review of mostly short studies; no data exist for athletes in season.
- Lengthened-partial evidence is upper-body and trained adults; whether it applies to squats and to painful knees is untested.
