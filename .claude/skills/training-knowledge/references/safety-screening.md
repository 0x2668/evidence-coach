# Safety: screening, red flags, scope of practice, PEDs, privacy

Evidence levels: consensus, meta-analysis, single study, expert opinion. IDs in brackets, such as [STR-05], point to the evidence table at the end of this file. Generated from the verified research notes on 2026-10-30; do not edit by hand.

## Practical rules

### Screening at intake
- Screen every new athlete with the PAR-Q+ (2025 version): 7 yes/no general health questions, including chest pain at rest, in daily life or during activity, and dizziness with loss of balance or loss of consciousness in the last 12 months. All NO means cleared for activity. [SAF-01, SAF-02]
- A YES to any of the 7 questions does not ban exercise; it leads to the condition-specific follow-up questions. A YES on a follow-up question means: before becoming more active or doing a fitness test, get further information (ePARmed-X+ online and/or a qualified exercise professional), and if in doubt see a doctor. [SAF-03, SAF-04]
- Treat a clearance as valid for at most 12 months and repeat screening sooner whenever health changes (new symptoms, new diagnosis, new medication, injury, pregnancy). [SAF-07, SAF-06]
- Delay increases in training during a temporary illness such as a cold or fever. Route pregnancy to POP and return-after-illness rules to MON. [SAF-06]
- For newly cleared people, start with 20-60 minutes of low-to-moderate intensity on 3-5 days per week, including aerobic and strength work, and build up gradually. [SAF-08]
- Use the ACSM logic to decide whether medical clearance is needed. It rests on three factors: current activity level, signs or symptoms and/or known cardiovascular, metabolic or renal disease, and the intended intensity. Do not count risk factors instead. [SAF-09, SAF-10]
- Count someone as a regular exerciser only if they have done planned, structured moderate exercise for at least 30 min on at least 3 days per week for the last 3 months. [SAF-11]
- Vigorous means at least 60% of heart-rate or VO2 reserve, at least 6 METs or RPE 14 or higher; moderate is 40 to under 60% HRR, 3 to under 6 METs, RPE 12-13. [SAF-12]
- Not exercising regularly: without symptoms or known disease, start light-to-moderate and progress gradually; with known cardiovascular, metabolic or renal disease, or any symptom, get medical clearance before any exercise. [SAF-13]
- Exercising regularly: without symptoms or disease, continue and progress gradually; with known disease and no symptoms, continue light-to-moderate but get clearance before vigorous; with any new symptom, stop and get medical clearance before continuing at any intensity. [SAF-14]
- Ask about these symptoms at intake and in check-ins, because they trigger medical clearance: chest, neck, jaw or arm pain or discomfort; breathlessness at rest or on mild exertion; dizziness or fainting; breathlessness lying down or at night; ankle swelling; palpitations or racing heart; calf pain on walking; known heart murmur; unusual fatigue or breathlessness in usual activities. [SAF-15]
- Explain why: exercise is safe for most people and lowers cardiac risk overall, but vigorous exercise briefly raises the risk of cardiac events in susceptible, unfit people, and those events are often preceded by warning symptoms. Unaccustomed people should build up gradually. [SAF-10, SAF-16]

### Chest pain and other emergencies during exercise
- If an athlete reports chest pain that feels tight or squeezing, chest pain spreading to the arms, neck or jaw, severe difficulty breathing, or someone collapses and does not respond normally: tell them to stop now and call 112 (EU; 999 in the UK) immediately. [SAF-17, SAF-21]
- While waiting: stop all activity, sit and rest supported; do not drive to hospital; if the person stops responding and stops breathing, bystanders start CPR. [SAF-18, SAF-19]
- Sudden unusual breathlessness, or chest pain with nausea or vomiting, or with a racing, slow, skipping or fluttering heartbeat, needs same-day urgent medical advice even if it does not meet the emergency criteria; the athlete does not train until assessed. [SAF-20, SAF-14]
- 112 is free, works everywhere in the EU from fixed and mobile phones and connects to ambulance, fire brigade or police. Always give 112 to EU users, not a national number from memory. [SAF-21]

### Knee pain during squats (and other joint pain)
- Never name a cause. Knee pain has many possible causes and even the NHS tells people not to self-diagnose; patellofemoral pain, the common pattern of pain around the kneecap with squats, stairs or running, is a clinical diagnosis with no definitive test. Say "this needs to be checked by a physiotherapist or doctor" when a referral trigger is present, and otherwise describe pain, not pathology. [SAF-25, SAF-28, SAF-29]
- First ask the red-flag questions: did it start with an injury (twist, collision, pop)? Can you put weight on it and move it? Is it badly swollen or misshapen? Does it lock or give way, or click painfully? Fever, feeling hot, cold or shivery, or a red or hot knee? Any YES means stop loading that leg and get urgent medical advice the same day. Painless clicking is normal. [SAF-22, SAF-23]
- Kneecap dislocation in the past, or a feeling that the kneecap slips, means referral to a physiotherapist rather than programme tweaks. [SAF-29]
- After an acute ankle or foot injury, inability to bear weight immediately or to take four steps means the person needs medical assessment for a possible fracture. The rule is sensitive (0.91) but not specific (0.25), so it signals "get it checked", not "it is broken". [SAF-26, SAF-27]
- The same logic holds for the knee: after an acute knee injury, inability to bear weight is a referral trigger. The Ottawa knee rule that clinicians use has pooled sensitivity 0.99 and specificity 0.49 for fracture (earlier review: 98.5% and 48.6%), so it rules a fracture out well but does not confirm one. The coach never applies the rule or palpates the knee; the weight-bearing question only decides "get it assessed today". [SAF-89, SAF-88, SAF-22]
- Pain without red flags that does not improve within a few weeks: recommend a GP or physiotherapist appointment. Do not wait for it to "settle on its own": patellofemoral pain is often stubborn and can last for many years. [SAF-24, SAF-90]
- Continue-with-modification criterion when there are no red flags: pain during or right after the session stays at or below 5/10 on a 0-to-10 scale and is back to the pre-session level by the next morning. If it exceeds that, reduce load, range or volume (STR for the substitutions); if it keeps exceeding it, refer. [SAF-30, SAF-31]
- Tell the athlete honestly that some pain during exercise is not automatically harmful: in chronic musculoskeletal pain, allowing some pain was no worse than pain-free exercise and slightly better in the short term (effect size -0.27). This applies to pain already assessed by a clinician, not to new or acute pain. [SAF-32]

### Back pain and spinal red flags
- Back pain with pain, tingling, weakness or numbness in both legs, loss of feeling around the genitals or anus, or new bladder or bowel problems: call 112 (999 in the UK) or go to the emergency department now. Same for back pain with chest pain or after a serious accident. These are possible cauda equina signs, and all 9 international pathways treat saddle numbness and bladder or bowel dysfunction as needing urgent imaging. [SAF-33, SAF-36, SAF-21]
- Back pain with feeling hot, cold, shivery or generally unwell, or severe pain that starts suddenly or worsens quickly: urgent same-day medical appointment, no training. [SAF-34]
- Back pain with unexplained weight loss, a lump or change of shape, pain that does not ease with rest or is worse at night, or no improvement after a few weeks: refer to a GP soon; training may continue only within comfort until seen. [SAF-35]
- Do not interpret red flags yourself. Most single red flags have little diagnostic value and serious spinal pathology is rare, so a red flag means "refer", never "you have X". [SAF-37, SAF-38]

### Heat: exertional heat stroke
- Suspect exertional heat stroke in anyone exercising hard in the heat who collapses or becomes confused, aggressive, irrational, drowsy or has a seizure. Altered behaviour is the key sign; core temperature above 40.5 C confirms it, but treat as heat stroke even at 40 C or if temperature is unknown. [SAF-39, SAF-40]
- Do not trust oral, ear, forehead or armpit thermometers after exercise in heat; a normal reading does not rule out heat stroke. [SAF-45]
- Action: stop activity, call 112, check responsiveness and breathing (collapse can be cardiac), and start the fastest whole-body cooling available at once: cold-water immersion up to the neck if possible, otherwise continuous cold-water dousing with fanning or ice-water towels. [SAF-41, SAF-40, SAF-46, SAF-21]
- Cooling first matters: immersion within 30 minutes of collapse was associated with 0% deaths in more than 2000 patients. The goal is below 38.9 C within 30 minutes; without a rectal thermometer, cool for 10-15 min and then hand over to emergency services. [SAF-42, SAF-43, SAF-44]
- After heat stroke, do not program a return yourself: it requires a 7- to 21-day rest, normal blood tests and physician clearance, then a gradual build-up. [SAF-47]

### Exercise-associated hyponatremia (long endurance events)
- In events and long sessions (marathon, ultra, long rides, hiking), nausea, weakness, dizziness, headache and swollen hands or feet can be early hyponatremia; confusion, vomiting, seizures, agitation or collapse are an emergency: call 112. [SAF-49, SAF-21]
- Hyponatremia (blood sodium below 135 mmol/L, up to 24 h after the activity) looks like heat illness and can only be confirmed with a blood test, so a symptomatic athlete goes to medical staff; do not advise them to drink more water. [SAF-48]
- It is caused mainly by too much water relative to sodium, for example drinking more than is sweated, and mild cases can worsen fast. Fluid and sodium targets are in NUT. [SAF-50]

### Exertional rhabdomyolysis
- Warn athletes, especially beginners, about new high-volume or unaccustomed sessions (many eccentric reps, "challenge" workouts, hot conditions, stimulant supplements): these promote rhabdomyolysis. Match new work to current fitness. [SAF-53]
- Distinguish from normal soreness: pain out of proportion to the session, muscle swelling, marked loss of range of motion, weakness, cola-coloured urine, or soreness that worsens or lasts beyond 5-7 days. Any of these means medical assessment; dark urine within 24 hours of hard exercise means same-day assessment. [SAF-51, SAF-52]
- Do not reassure an athlete that it is "just DOMS" when warning signs are present; diagnosis needs a blood test (creatine kinase of at least 5,000 IU/L plus symptoms). [SAF-54]

### Head impact: falls in climbing, cycling crashes
- After any head impact, call 112 if any red flag is present: neck pain or tenderness, severe or increasing headache, worsening consciousness, double vision, seizure, vomiting, weakness or tingling or burning in arms or legs, loss of consciousness, or increasing restlessness or agitation. [SAF-55, SAF-21]
- Do not move the person (except to keep the airway open) and do not remove the helmet unless trained; a spinal injury must be assessed first. [SAF-58]
- Any suspected concussion: stop the session immediately, and no return to training until assessed medically, even if symptoms go away. [SAF-56]
- Aftercare: not alone for at least the first 1-2 hours, no alcohol or drugs, not sent home alone, no driving until cleared by a health professional. [SAF-57]

### Fatigue: overreaching versus overtraining syndrome
- Planned hard blocks can produce functional overreaching, a short performance dip that turns into improvement after recovery; this is normal. [SAF-59]
- Fatigue and underperformance lasting weeks to months despite reduced load is non-functional overreaching or possibly overtraining syndrome. The coach cannot tell them apart; refer to a physician, because the diagnosis requires excluding illness, infection, energy deficit, iron deficiency and other causes, and no single marker is accepted. [SAF-61, SAF-59, SAF-60]

### REDs and disordered eating: recognise and refer
- The coach's job is to notice and refer, never to diagnose or to set weight-loss targets for an at-risk athlete. Body-composition goals should be decided "health first, performance second" by trained health staff after screening for body-image concerns and problematic eating. [SAF-63, SAF-65]
- Watch for warning signs the athlete may report or that show in the data: missed periods (3-11 consecutive missed cycles is a primary REDs indicator; cycles longer than 35 days a secondary one), bone stress injuries (at least 1 high-risk or 2 other bone stress injuries within 2 years), falling off the growth curve in young athletes, and a known eating disorder or past disordered eating. [SAF-66, SAF-67, SAF-68, SAF-70, SAF-62, SAF-69]
- Be aware that coaches and beliefs that a certain weight improves performance are common drivers of disordered eating; avoid weight-centred feedback. [SAF-62, SAF-63]
- Screening questionnaires are cheap first-line tools but less sensitive; the IOC tool must not be used alone or as a diagnosis. When signs are present, refer to a sports physician (ideally with a sports dietitian); do not score questionnaires such as the EDE-Q yourself. [SAF-64, SAF-65, SAF-69]

### Scope of practice
- Program independently only for low- and moderate-risk people identified by screening. For higher-risk people with stable, managed conditions, work only from documented guidance by their treating health professional. [SAF-72]
- Do not diagnose, do not run diagnostic tests, do not provide therapeutic treatment or independent rehabilitation, do not give nutrition advice beyond general healthy-eating guidelines, and do not counsel psychologically. Treat advice on medicines or painkillers as treatment and leave it to health professionals. [SAF-73]
- Referral is part of the job, not a failure: refer, and ask the athlete to share the professional's guidance so the programme can follow it. [SAF-74]

### Anabolic-androgenic steroids and SARMs
- Refuse to give dosing, cycling, sourcing or "safe use" advice for steroids or SARMs. Explain risks and status instead. [SAF-75, SAF-80]
- Risks to state: higher risk of death and of heart, psychiatric, hormonal, liver, kidney and musculoskeletal disorders; heart attack, stroke and blood clots; liver injury; acne, hair loss, aggression, depression, sexual dysfunction and testicular shrinkage; dependence in about one in three users (32.5%); suppressed natural testosterone with weeks or months of hypogonadism after stopping. [SAF-75, SAF-78, SAF-76, SAF-77]
- SARMs have no FDA-approved use, are linked to liver injury, myocarditis and tendon rupture, and online products are often contaminated; products sold as supplements may secretly contain steroids. [SAF-80, SAF-78]
- Anti-doping status: all anabolic agents (WADA class S1, including steroids and SARMs such as ostarine, LGD-4033 and RAD140) are prohibited at all times, in and out of competition. [SAF-81, SAF-82]
- If the athlete already uses them, advise a doctor's consultation promptly and do not tell them to stop abruptly on their own, because withdrawal can be dangerous. Stay non-judgemental so they keep reporting symptoms (chest pain, breathlessness, yellow skin, dark urine mean urgent care). [SAF-79, SAF-77]

### Privacy: athlete data (EU GDPR)
- Treat injuries, symptoms, illness, menstrual data, screening answers and medical clearances as "data concerning health" (GDPR Art. 4(15)), a special category whose processing is prohibited unless an exception applies, typically the athlete's explicit consent for specified purposes (Art. 9(1)-(2)(a)). [SAF-83, SAF-85]
- When a human coach wants to share athlete data (with another coach, a club, an app or this agent), share only what is necessary for the stated purpose (data minimisation, Art. 5(1)(c)), prefer summaries or pseudonymised data, and do not pass on health data without the athlete's explicit consent for that purpose. [SAF-84, SAF-85, SAF-86]
- Design by default: collect only the data each purpose needs, keep it no longer than needed, and limit who can access it (Art. 25(2)). [SAF-86]
- Consent can be withdrawn at any time, and withdrawing must be as easy as giving it (Art. 7(3)). [SAF-87]
- These are legal texts, not coaching evidence; for a concrete compliance question, say that the coach needs legal advice. [SAF-83, SAF-85]

## Red flags and referral triggers

### Stop now and call 112 (EU; 999 in the UK)
- Chest pain that is tight or squeezing or spreads to arm, neck or jaw; severe difficulty breathing; collapse with abnormal responsiveness. Stop, sit supported, do not drive; start CPR if not breathing. [SAF-17, SAF-18, SAF-19, SAF-21]
- Collapse or confusion, aggression or irrational behaviour while exercising in the heat: call 112 and cool immediately (cold-water immersion or the fastest alternative). [SAF-39, SAF-41, SAF-21]
- Confusion, vomiting, seizure or collapse during or after a long endurance event (possible severe hyponatremia). [SAF-49, SAF-21]
- After head impact: neck pain, severe or increasing headache, worsening consciousness, double vision, seizure, vomiting, limb weakness or tingling, loss of consciousness, increasing agitation. Do not move the person or remove the helmet unless trained. [SAF-55, SAF-58]
- Back pain with problems in both legs, saddle numbness, or new bladder or bowel dysfunction. [SAF-33, SAF-36]

### Stop training and get medical advice the same day
- Sudden unusual breathlessness; chest pain with nausea or vomiting; racing, slow, skipping or fluttering heartbeat. [SAF-20]
- Any new symptom from the ACSM list (chest discomfort, breathlessness on mild exertion, dizziness or fainting, palpitations, ankle swelling, calf pain on walking, unusual fatigue): stop and get clearance before any further training. [SAF-14, SAF-15]
- Suspected concussion even without red flags: removed from activity until medically assessed. [SAF-56]
- Knee: cannot bear weight or move it, badly swollen or misshapen, locking or giving way, painful clicking, or fever with a red or hot joint. Inability to bear weight after a knee injury is backed by the Ottawa knee rule reviews (sensitivity 0.99 for fracture) as a reason to get imaging-level assessment, never as a diagnosis. Ankle or foot: unable to take four steps after injury. [SAF-22, SAF-23, SAF-89, SAF-88, SAF-26]
- Back pain with fever or feeling generally unwell, or severe pain that is sudden or rapidly worsening. [SAF-34]
- Dark or cola-coloured urine within 24 hours of hard exercise, or muscle pain out of proportion with swelling or weakness. [SAF-51, SAF-52]

### Refer soon (GP, physiotherapist, sports physician) and adapt training meanwhile
- Joint or back pain that does not improve within a few weeks (knee pain with squats can persist for years if left); back pain worse at night or not eased by rest; unexplained weight loss. [SAF-24, SAF-35, SAF-90]
- Soreness that persists or worsens beyond 5-7 days after a session. [SAF-51]
- Fatigue and underperformance lasting weeks to months despite reduced load. [SAF-61, SAF-60]
- REDs warning signs: missed or irregular periods, bone stress injuries, falling off the growth curve, signs of disordered eating. [SAF-66, SAF-67, SAF-68, SAF-70, SAF-64]
- Any PAR-Q+ follow-up YES, a health change since the last screening, or a client in a higher-risk category. [SAF-04, SAF-07, SAF-72]
- Athlete reports using steroids, SARMs or "bodybuilding" products. [SAF-79]

### Continue with modification (only if none of the above applies)
- Pain stays at or below 5/10 during and right after the session and is back to baseline by the next morning: continue, adjusting load, range or volume; if it exceeds this, reduce; if it keeps exceeding it or lasts a few weeks, refer. [SAF-30, SAF-24]
- Planned short-term fatigue that resolves with recovery (functional overreaching): continue the plan with recovery days. [SAF-59]

## Contested areas

### Training through pain
- Position A: rest from pain-provoking activity or train only pain-free (the comparison arms in the trials). [SAF-31]
- Position B: allow pain up to 5/10 that settles by the next morning; in one RCT this was no worse than 6 weeks of rest, and in chronic pain, exercise into some pain gave a small short-term benefit. [SAF-30, SAF-31, SAF-32]
- Why they differ: evidence for B is small (one small RCT, a feasibility pilot and a small meta-analysis) and comes from diagnosed tendinopathy and chronic pain, not from new, undiagnosed pain. [SAF-30, SAF-31, SAF-32]
- What the agent should do: use the pain-monitoring rule only after the red-flag questions are all negative; for new pain that persists, refer. Tell the athlete: "Some discomfort during rehab-type training is acceptable and not a sign of damage if it stays moderate and settles by tomorrow, but the evidence is limited, so if it gets worse we get it checked."

### Is REDs a diagnosable syndrome?
- Position A (IOC 2023): REDs is assessed through screening, a traffic-light risk assessment and physician diagnosis. [SAF-64, SAF-65]
- Position B (critics): energy availability cannot be measured in the field and symptoms are generic, so labelling "REDs" risks missing other causes. [SAF-71]
- What the agent should do: both positions agree that the coach should not diagnose. Report the warning signs, refer to a physician, and never use the label "REDs" for an athlete.

## Evidence gaps

- No authoritative European or international scope-of-practice statement for fitness professionals was found; the scope rules rest on one national register (AUSactive, Australia, 2025). No source explicitly names medication advice; the rule treats it as treatment.
- Acute knee-injury red flags come from NHS patient information (expert-opinion level); the Ottawa knee rule reviews support the weight-bearing trigger only indirectly, because the saved abstracts do not list the rule items. No validated knee referral rule for coaches was found.
- The NHS pages (heart attack, knee pain, back pain) are patient information, not formal guidelines, so every claim taken from them is rated expert opinion; the cardiac red flags have no guideline-level source with a quotable text in this knowledge base (the 2020 ESC sports cardiology guideline was saved without text).
- The pain-monitoring threshold is not validated for undiagnosed pain in healthy trainees.
- Night pain and unexplained weight loss as red flags have poor diagnostic accuracy in the research literature; they are kept as refer-soon triggers because official guidance lists them.
- Hyponatremia and rhabdomyolysis guidance comes partly from military and wilderness-medicine settings and a narrative review; recreational-athlete data are sparse.
- GDPR: whether a coaching service may rely on a legal basis other than explicit consent for health data is a legal question this knowledge base does not answer.
