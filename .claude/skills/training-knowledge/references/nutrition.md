# Sports nutrition within a coach's scope

Evidence levels: consensus, meta-analysis, single study, expert opinion. IDs in brackets, such as [STR-05], point to the evidence table at the end of this file. Generated from the verified research notes on 2026-10-30; do not edit by hand.

## Practical rules

### Daily protein and distribution
- For healthy adults who train, set the daily protein target at 1.4-2.0 g/kg body weight (ISSN) or 1.2-2.0 g/kg for athletes in general (ACSM/AND/DC); most people reach it from food. [NUT-01, NUT-02]
- For hypertrophy in resistance-trained adults, aim around 1.6 g/kg/day; 2.2 g/kg/day is the upper limit worth suggesting to those who want to maximise gains. Gains plateaued near 1.6 g/kg/day in trials, but the break point was not statistically significant and the trials were mostly in young men. [NUT-03, NUT-09]
- Distribute protein across the day in regular servings of about 0.3 g/kg (ACSM/AND/DC) or 20-40 g (ISSN), roughly every 3-4 hours, including after training. Treat this as a sensible default, not a hard ceiling: total daily intake matters more than the split. [NUT-02, NUT-05, NUT-08]
- If an athlete prefers fewer, larger protein meals, do not forbid it. One acute study in healthy young men found a 100 g dose after resistance exercise gave a larger and longer response than 25 g; this does not yet show better long-term muscle gain and may not extend beyond young men. [NUT-06, NUT-07]
- For lean, resistance-trained adults in an energy deficit, protein can be higher: 1.9-3.2 g/kg/day (ISSN 2026) or 2.3-3.1 g/kg/day (ISSN 2017). The evidence is few, short studies in lean trained people, so use the lower part of the range and keep resistance training in place. [NUT-09, NUT-70, NUT-08]
- Set expectations for protein powder: in trials it added only about 0.30 kg fat-free mass and about 2.49 kg to 1RM over training alone, with smaller effects in older people. Tell athletes food and training come first and powder is a convenience. [NUT-04]
- Adults over 65: at least 1.0-1.2 g/kg/day, and at least 1.2 g/kg/day if they exercise; ESPEN gives the same 1.0-1.2 g/kg/day for healthy older people and says all older people should do daily physical activity or exercise. Do not give higher-protein advice to anyone with severe kidney disease (eGFR below 30 mL/min/1.73 m2); refer to their doctor. [NUT-10, NUT-81, NUT-83, NUT-11]
- Older people who are malnourished or at risk because of acute or chronic illness: ESPEN advises 1.2-1.5 g/kg/day, more with severe illness or injury. This is a clinical decision, so refer to a doctor or dietitian and do not set it yourself. [NUT-82, NUT-66]
- Timing is secondary to the daily total. ISSN calls meeting total daily protein, preferably in evenly spaced feedings about every 3 h, the primary emphasis, and says high-quality protein from immediately to 2 h after exercise stimulates muscle protein synthesis. Suggest a protein-containing meal soon after training as a convenience, not as a closing window. [NUT-73, NUT-74, NUT-08]

### Carbohydrate by training load and during exercise
- Scale daily carbohydrate to the training load: 3-5 g/kg/day for light or skill-based training; 5-7 g/kg/day for a moderate programme of about 1 h/day; 6-10 g/kg/day for 1-3 h/day of moderate-high intensity; 8-12 g/kg/day for extreme commitment above 4-5 h/day. These are targets for days when training quality matters; on easy days intake can follow energy goals. [NUT-12]
- Before exercise lasting more than 60 minutes, eat 1-4 g/kg of well-tolerated carbohydrate 1-4 hours beforehand. [NUT-16]
- During events longer than 60 minutes, take 30-60 g carbohydrate per hour. In events of 2.5 hours or more, up to 90 g/h is linked to better performance, but only train toward it in the gut first. [NUT-13, NUT-23]
- Above 60 g/h, use glucose plus fructose rather than a single sugar; a single source above that rate is likely to cause GI problems. [NUT-14]
- Practise the race-day eating and drinking plan in training and build up gradually to the planned carbohydrate rate. The dose and timeline of gut training are not established. [NUT-23, NUT-22]
- Carbohydrate loading is for events of more than 90 minutes only: 10-12 g/kg/day for 36-48 hours before. Do not prescribe it for shorter events. [NUT-15]
- When two fuel-demanding sessions are close together, eat about 1.0-1.2 g/kg/h of carbohydrate starting early in recovery for 4-6 hours; ISSN gives 1.2 g/kg/h when recovery time is under 4 h. With a full day between sessions, daily totals matter instead. [NUT-17, NUT-72]

### Fuelling, fluid and sodium for a long ultra (test scenario)
- State the rate first as a range and a test: for ultra-marathons, ISSN suggests 150-400 kcal/h, of which 30-50 g carbohydrate and 5-10 g protein per hour, from varied, calorie-dense foods, with more savoury foods as the race gets longer. [NUT-18]
- For the carbohydrate target, give the athlete a working range of 30-60 g/h to start, and raise toward the higher end only if rehearsed in training and tolerated. The 90 g/h guidance may be unrealistic in ultras longer than 6 hours and can cause malabsorption and GI distress. This is contested between ACSM and ISSN. [NUT-13, NUT-18, NUT-19]
- Fluid: a typical plan is 0.4-0.8 L/h; for ultras, ISSN gives about 450-750 mL/h (150-250 mL every 20 min). Adjust to sweat rate, heat and body size, and never drink beyond sweat losses. [NUT-27, NUT-20, NUT-30]
- Sodium: ISSN notes that sodium above 575 mg per litre of fluid may be needed to reduce hyponatraemia risk, more than most commercial drinks provide. Take sodium when sweat rate is high (above 1.2 L/h), sweat is salty, or exercise lasts longer than 2 hours. [NUT-20, NUT-28]
- If sweat rate and tolerance are unknown, start with drinking to thirst plus a planned sodium source, because thirst alone may supply too little sodium. Weigh before and after long training sessions to estimate sweat rate. [NUT-21, NUT-25, NUT-32]
- Gut problems: rehearse the plan in training; a low-FODMAP diet is a restrictive therapeutic diet, so refer to a dietitian rather than set it up yourself. Weight gain during a race means over-drinking. [NUT-22, NUT-31]
- If an athlete gains weight during a long event or has symptoms after over-drinking, treat it as possible exercise-associated hyponatremia and follow the SAF cluster for the emergency. Sodium below 135 mmol/L defines it. [NUT-30, NUT-31]

### Hydration
- Pre-hydrate with about 5-10 mL/kg in the 2-4 hours before exercise, aiming for pale yellow urine. [NUT-26]
- Aim to keep body mass loss during exercise below 2%, and never gain weight. Plain water is enough for sessions under 1 hour. [NUT-24, NUT-31, NUT-34]
- Individualise: sweat rates differ widely, so estimate by weighing before and after exercise. [NUT-25]
- Choosing between plan and thirst: use a planned drinking strategy for activities longer than 90 min (especially in the heat), higher-intensity exercise with high sweat rates, when performance matters, and when carbohydrate intake of 1 g/min is wanted. Drinking to thirst may be enough for exercise under 1 h to 90 min, cooler conditions and lower intensity. Athletes with high sweat rates or performance goals should measure sweat rate under competition-like conditions and tailor drinking to keep body mass loss under 2%. Never drink so much that weight is gained. [NUT-75, NUT-76, NUT-77]
- Drinking to thirst is a safe default for recreational athletes and when sweat rate is unknown. In 1-2 hour cycling or running in temperate-to-warm conditions, drinking to thirst (about 2.1% body mass loss) performed as well as a programmed plan (about 1.0% loss). For competitive athletes, thirst may lag behind needs, so use a planned strategy based on measured sweat rate. This is contested. [NUT-32, NUT-35, NUT-33]
- After exercise with a fluid deficit, drink about 125-150% of the deficit (1.25-1.5 L per kg lost), with sodium from food or drinks. [NUT-29]
- Over-drinking, not sweat loss, is the main cause of exercise-associated hyponatremia, mostly in slower recreational athletes. Do not tell anyone to drink as much as possible. [NUT-30]

### Weight loss: safe rate, and the crash diet request (test scenario)
- For athletes who want to lose weight, cap the rate at 0.5-1.0% of body weight per week to protect lean mass, with resistance training and enough protein continuing. One small RCT supports the slower rate (0.7% versus 1.4% per week). [NUT-41, NUT-42, NUT-08]
- Size the deficit moderately: about 250-500 kcal/day below periodised needs, for a short-term goal over about 3-6 weeks. [NUT-43]
- When someone asks for a crash diet of around a thousand kcal/day, decline to write it. Such an intake sits in the low-energy band (800-1200 kcal/day, and under 800 for very-low-energy diets), which gives faster short-term loss, no long-term advantage over moderate restriction, and may increase lean mass loss and nutrient shortfalls. [NUT-44]
- Explain that clinical guidance reserves these diets for supervised care: NICE allows 800-1,200 kcal/day only within a specialist service for people with obesity, under 800 kcal/day only with a clinically assessed need to lose weight rapidly, nutritionally complete, for no more than 12 weeks with clinical supervision. NICE also advises against nutritionally unbalanced restrictive diets as ineffective long term and potentially harmful. [NUT-45, NUT-46, NUT-47, NUT-48]
- Offer the safe alternative instead: a moderate deficit of 250-500 kcal/day, 0.5-1.0% of body weight per week at most, resistance training and the protein targets above, and a review after 3-6 weeks. [NUT-43, NUT-41, NUT-09]
- Refer: any diet that holds an energy deficit should come with support from a registered dietitian or registered nutritionist. Offer the referral in the same message as the decline, and refer to a doctor if the person has a medical condition, eating-disorder history or is very restrictive already. [NUT-49, NUT-66]
- Do not pressure athletes about body weight or composition; coach comments on weight are a recognised risk. Keep goals performance- and health-based. [NUT-40]

### Energy availability and RED-S (nutrition side)
- Explain RED-S as the IOC does: harmful health and performance outcomes in female and male athletes exposed to low energy availability, meaning too little energy intake for the exercise energy expended. [NUT-36]
- Do not calculate or quote a fixed energy availability cut-off to an athlete. The 30 kcal/kg FFM/day figure came from short laboratory studies in a small sample of sedentary women, and IOC 2023 warns that setting a definitive clinical threshold is risky; the older ACSM/AND/DC figures (45 and 30 kcal/kg FFM/day) are not fixed clinical cut-offs. [NUT-37, NUT-38, NUT-39]
- Do not combine a weight-loss goal with a high training load without checking fuelling: use the deficit limits above and refer if red flags appear. [NUT-36, NUT-41, NUT-43]

### Supplements: what the coach may mention
- Food first. A complete nutritional assessment, including medical history, biochemical testing and checks for medicine interactions, comes before any supplement decision; that is a dietitian or doctor task. [NUT-51, NUT-71, NUT-59]
- Only a few performance supplements have good evidence in high-performance athletes: caffeine, creatine, specific buffering agents and nitrate. The coach may mention caffeine and creatine for adults and suggest trying them in training, not first in competition. [NUT-50]
- Caffeine for adults: 3-6 mg/kg, commonly about 60 min before exercise; as little as 2 mg/kg may work and 9 mg/kg causes frequent side effects without extra benefit. Sleep disruption and anxiety vary between people. [NUT-55]
- Creatine monohydrate for healthy adults: 3-5 g/day saturates stores over 3-4 weeks, or load at about 0.3 g/kg/day for 5-7 days first. Warn about a short-term weight gain from fluid retention of about 0.5-1.0 L. ISSN reports no compelling harm in healthy people at up to 30 g/day for up to 5 years, but its authors have industry ties. [NUT-56, NUT-57, NUT-58]
- Never recommend high-dose iron unless iron deficiency has been diagnosed; refer for blood tests. Vitamin and mineral supplements, including vitamin D, are for diagnosed or likely deficiency. [NUT-54, NUT-59]
- Vegetarian and vegan athletes are at risk of low intake of energy, protein, creatine, n-3 fatty acids, iron, calcium, zinc and vitamin B12; prompt a dietitian review rather than prescribing supplements. [NUT-69, NUT-65]
- Do not discourage a vegan diet. A review argues that with strategic food choices and appropriate supplementation it can meet the needs of most athletes; the points to check are energy and protein sufficiency, vitamin B12, iron, zinc, calcium, iodine, vitamin D, and EPA and DHA. [NUT-79, NUT-78]
- For vegan athletes the same review suggests protein of 1.7-2.0 g/kg, and 1.8-2.7 g/kg in weight-loss phases, from varied plant foods. This is expert opinion above the 1.4-2.0 g/kg default, so treat it as the upper part of the range and leave supplement choices to a dietitian. [NUT-80, NUT-01]
- Do not advise ketogenic or very low carbohydrate diets for performance: ISSN reports largely neutral or detrimental effects (fat oxidation about 1.5 g/min), and all studies in elite athletes of up to six weeks found worse performance. [NUT-67, NUT-68]

### Contamination and anti-doping
- Warn every athlete, especially anyone under anti-doping rules, that supplements can contain undeclared substances: in the original survey about 15% of over 600 supplements contained undeclared prohormones. The sample was not random, so the true rate is uncertain. [NUT-52]
- Third-party testing schemes reduce the risk but do not remove it. If an athlete in a tested sport wants any supplement, tell them to use a third-party tested product and to check with their anti-doping authority and doctor; the coach does not clear products. [NUT-53, NUT-51]
- The coach does not recommend, sell or dose any other supplement (including prohormones, fat burners and unlisted blends). Refer to a sports dietitian or doctor. [NUT-50, NUT-51, NUT-52]

### Adolescent athlete who wants to "bulk" (test scenario)
- Food first, no supplements. Sports Dietitians Australia advises adolescent athletes to meet nutrient needs from core foods, and a 2021 review advises that young athletes should not be encouraged to use performance supplements. [NUT-61, NUT-63]
- Do not recommend protein powder, creatine or weight gainers. The ISSN minority view that creatine might be considered for supervised, serious young athletes conflicts with the other sources and has authors with industry ties; the coach does not act on it. [NUT-64, NUT-61, NUT-63]
- Protein: about 1.5 g/kg/day is sufficient for adolescent athletes provided total energy is adequate, for example about 0.3 g/kg across 5 eating occasions. Extra protein without enough energy will not build muscle. [NUT-62]
- Match eating to daily training, spread quality carbohydrate and protein over the day (especially right after training), and watch calcium, vitamin D and iron. Clinical supplements (calcium, iron, vitamin D) are only under a doctor's or sports dietitian's guidance. [NUT-60, NUT-63]
- Explain that adequate total energy with regular meals is the precondition for gaining muscle. If the adolescent is skipping meals, very worried about weight or has a medical issue, refer to a doctor or sports dietitian. [NUT-62, NUT-60, NUT-66]

### Scope of practice: coach versus registered dietitian
- The coach gives general, food-based guidance from the rules above: protein, carbohydrate and fluid targets, gut training, and basic education on supplements and contamination. [NUT-02, NUT-12, NUT-23, NUT-51]
- Refer to a registered dietitian or registered nutritionist for a personalised nutrition plan. [NUT-65]
- Medical nutrition therapy is a sports dietitian role, not a coach role: eating disorders, food allergies, diabetes and gastrointestinal problems. [NUT-66]
- Refer to a doctor for suspected iron deficiency, severe kidney disease, and any medical condition that affects diet or supplements. [NUT-54, NUT-11, NUT-51]
- Any diet that holds an energy deficit should have registered dietitian or registered nutritionist support. [NUT-49]

## Red flags and referral triggers

- Request for a very-low-energy or low-energy diet (800-1200 kcal/day or lower): decline, explain, offer the moderate alternative, refer to a dietitian or, for obesity, a specialist service. [NUT-44, NUT-45, NUT-46, NUT-49]
- Nutritionally unbalanced restrictive diets: advise against and refer. [NUT-48, NUT-49]
- Possible low energy availability or RED-S: an athlete who combines high training load, weight loss pressure and restrictive eating. Do not diagnose or use a numeric threshold; refer to a doctor or sports dietitian. [NUT-36, NUT-38, NUT-37]
- Eating disorder, food allergy, diabetes or GI problems: medical nutrition therapy; refer. [NUT-66]
- Coach or entourage pressure on weight: the coach must not add to it; flag the risk. [NUT-40]
- Severe kidney disease (eGFR below 30 mL/min/1.73 m2): no higher-protein advice; refer. [NUT-11]
- Suspected iron deficiency or request for high-dose iron: needs blood tests and clinical follow-up. [NUT-54]
- Over-drinking with weight gain or hyponatremia symptoms during long events: sodium below 135 mmol/L; emergency handling is in SAF. [NUT-30, NUT-31]
- Persistent GI distress during long events: gut training first; low-FODMAP only with a dietitian. [NUT-22]
- Athlete in a tested sport asking about a supplement: third-party tested product and anti-doping check. [NUT-52, NUT-53]
- Adolescent asking for supplements or rapid weight change: food first; refer to a doctor or sports dietitian. [NUT-61, NUT-63]
- Vegan or vegetarian athlete with a restrictive or low-energy diet: dietitian review. [NUT-69]

## Contested areas

### Per-meal protein ceiling
- Position A: about 0.25 g/kg (20-40 g) every 3-4 hours is the general recommendation. [NUT-05, NUT-02]
- Position B: a tracer study found 100 g produced a larger and longer response than 25 g after resistance exercise, challenging the ceiling; however it is an acute study in 12 healthy young men per group. [NUT-06, NUT-07]
- What the agent should do: use spread-out servings as the default for convenience and total daily intake as the main target. Tell the athlete the per-meal "ceiling" is not a hard limit and that daily total matters most. [NUT-08]

### Carbohydrate per hour in ultras
- Position A: ACSM/AND/DC, 30-60 g/h in events over 60 min and up to 90 g/h from 2.5 h. [NUT-13]
- Position B: ISSN ultra stand, 150-400 kcal/h with 30-50 g/h carbohydrate, calling 90 g/h unrealistic beyond 6 hours. [NUT-18, NUT-19]
- What the agent should do: start at 30-60 g/h, rehearse in training, move higher only with tolerance. Explain that the higher figures may be unrealistic in the longest ultras. [NUT-13, NUT-19, NUT-23]

### Fluid and sodium strategy: drink to thirst or a plan
- Position A: drink to thirst is the best way to avoid under- and over-hydration; ad libitum drinking matched programmed drinking in 1-2 h events. [NUT-21, NUT-32, NUT-35]
- Position B: for competitive athletes thirst may lag behind needs; keep body mass loss below 2% and use a plan based on measured sweat rate; in ultras sodium may be too low with thirst alone. [NUT-24, NUT-31, NUT-33, NUT-20]
- Newer narrative review that reconciles them: plan for activities over 90 min, heat, high sweat rates or performance goals; thirst is enough for short, cool or easy sessions. [NUT-75, NUT-76, NUT-77]
- What the agent should do: recreational and unknown sweat rate, drink to thirst with a sodium source; competitive or long events, test sweat rate and make a plan, never exceeding sweat loss. Explain that the 2007 ACSM stand is older than the newer meta-analysis. [NUT-25, NUT-30]

### Energy availability thresholds
- Position A: 45 kcal/kg FFM/day for balance and below 30 for impairment. [NUT-39]
- Position B: IOC 2023 says the 30 figure came from small short studies in sedentary women and a definitive clinical threshold is risky. [NUT-37, NUT-38]
- What the agent should do: do not use thresholds as cut-offs; watch symptoms and context and refer. [NUT-36]

### Protein in an energy deficit
- Position A: 1.6-2.2 g/kg/day with resistance training; 1.9-3.2 g/kg/day for lean trained people in a deficit. [NUT-09]
- Position B: 2.3-3.1 g/kg/day (ISSN 2017). [NUT-70]
- What the agent should do: the ranges overlap; use the lower part and confirm resistance training is in place. Evidence is few short studies in lean trained people. [NUT-09, NUT-08]

### Ketogenic and low-carbohydrate diets
- Fat oxidation rises but performance is neutral or worse than with higher carbohydrate; long-term adaptation is still debated. [NUT-67, NUT-68]
- What the agent should do: do not recommend for performance; if the athlete insists, refer to a dietitian and keep key sessions fuelled. [NUT-67, NUT-12]

### Supplements in adolescents
- Position A: SDA and the 2021 review: no performance supplements. [NUT-61, NUT-63]
- Position B: ISSN minority view: creatine may be considered with supervision. [NUT-64]
- What the agent should do: follow position A. Explain that food covers the need when total energy is adequate. [NUT-62]

## Evidence gaps

- No claim lists specific signs or symptoms of low energy availability or RED-S, validated screening questionnaires, or return-to-training criteria. The agent can only describe the syndrome and refer. [NUT-36]
- No claim gives a sodium dose per hour in mg for the athlete during ultras, only a concentration (mg per litre) and when to take sodium. [NUT-20, NUT-28]
- No claim gives energy intake or weight-gain rates for growing adolescents who want to build muscle, so "bulk" advice is limited to food first and the protein figure. [NUT-62]
- Long-term gut-training dose and timeline are not established. [NUT-23]
- Evidence on caffeine and creatine in adolescents, and on any safe supplement in female athletes or pregnancy, is not in the claims.
- Per-meal protein dose beyond healthy young men is untested. [NUT-06, NUT-07]
- Ultra-specific hydration evidence is mostly observational; no claim covers hot ultras with drinking-to-thirst. [NUT-35, NUT-18]
- No claims on alcohol, practical meal plans, or energy needs for specific sports; these were not researched.
