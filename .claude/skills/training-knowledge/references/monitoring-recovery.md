# Load monitoring, recovery, sleep, injury prevention

Evidence levels: consensus, meta-analysis, single study, expert opinion. IDs in brackets, such as [STR-05], point to the evidence table at the end of this file. Generated from the verified research notes on 2026-10-30; do not edit by hand.

## Practical rules

### Internal and external load
- Record internal load for every session as session-RPE: after the session ask "How was your workout?" on the modified CR-10 scale (0 rest to 10 maximal), then multiply the rating by session duration in minutes, giving arbitrary units (AU). Example: 87 min at RPE 4 = 348 AU. [MON-01]
- Use session-RPE as the default internal-load measure across all disciplines (strength, running, cycling, climbing, general fitness). It has good validity and reliability across sports, sexes and ages (36 validation and reliability studies). Most of that validation is in endurance and team sports, so treat it as less proven for lifting and climbing. The original validation (cycling and basketball, against a heart-rate method) concluded it is valid across many types of exercise [MON-02, MON-51].
- Each week, compute training monotony (mean daily load divided by the standard deviation of daily load over the week) and strain (weekly load multiplied by monotony). When load is high and the days are uniform, suggest more contrast between hard and easy days. Never present a monotony or strain threshold as a validated illness or injury predictor. [MON-03]
- Combine external load (distance, duration, elevation, power, sets x reps x load) with internal load (session-RPE, heart rate). No single marker identifies maladaptation. [MON-04]
- Progress load in small steps rather than large jumps or drops, and individualise it: athletes differ a lot in how quickly they respond and adapt. [MON-05]

### Load spikes and injury: the defensible rule
- Do not use ACWR "sweet spot" thresholds (0.8-1.3, or claims that a high ratio multiplies injury risk) to predict injury or to tell an athlete they are at risk. The underlying evidence is observational, mostly from team sports, and methodologically disputed. If a coach asks about ACWR, explain it as at most one contextual indicator. Team-sport systematic reviews are split: one endorsed ACWR as one tool among others, another found most studies reporting higher risk at high ratios but with 14 different binning methods and 95% coupled calculation [MON-06, MON-07, MON-08, MON-09, MON-10, MON-52, MON-53, MON-54, MON-55, MON-56].
- Do flag sudden large spikes relative to what the athlete has recently done; this is the defensible core that both sides accept. Explain to the coach that a high ratio cannot tell whether acute load jumped or the chronic base is low, so look at the raw numbers. [MON-05, MON-08]
- For runners, check each run against the longest run of the previous 30 days. A run more than 10% longer was associated with a higher overuse-injury rate (hazard rate ratio 1.64 for spikes above 10-30%, 2.28 above 100%), whereas weekly ACWR and week-to-week change were not informative. Several consecutive 10% steps in one week can still be too much. [MON-11, MON-12]
- Do not sell the weekly "10% rule" as proven protection: in an RCT of novice runners, a graded programme based on the 10% rule produced the same injury incidence (20.8% vs 20.3%) as a standard programme. Use small progressions as a principle, not as a guarantee. [MON-13, MON-05]
- When a coach asks "how much is too much", answer that load metrics cannot quantify how a given load change shifts injury risk; progression is a judgement based on overload principles and the athlete's response (check-ins, pain, performance). [MON-07]

### HRV and resting heart rate
- If the athlete tracks HRV, use one consistent protocol: measure each morning after waking, in the same position, use RMSSD (usually log-transformed in apps), and judge it against a rolling 7-day average, not a single day. [MON-14, MON-24]
- A practical decision band: treat values within the individual baseline mean minus 1 SD (or mean plus/minus 0.5 SD) as normal. Act only on a sustained move outside the band combined with poor check-in answers. [MON-14, MON-18]
- Set expectations honestly. HRV-guided training improves vagal HRV (SMD 0.50) and may reduce the chance of a negative response, but its advantage for VO2max or performance over a well-designed fixed plan is small and not statistically significant (SMD about 0.20). It is an option, not a requirement. [MON-15, MON-16]
- Do not read a low HRV as fatigue when resting HR is also low in a highly trained endurance athlete; this pattern can reflect parasympathetic saturation. [MON-17]
- Trust only resting HRV from wearables; HRV recorded during exercise or movement is inaccurate. [MON-24] Portable devices differed from ECG with a small average error (effect size 0.23); the error depended on the HRV metric, body position and sex but not on the device, so keep position and metric constant for each athlete [MON-57, MON-58].
- If an endurance athlete wants HRV-guided plans, tell them the benefit is mainly for submaximal fitness markers (g 0.296), with small, non-significant gains in performance and VO2peak; one reason may be that HRV-guided plans contained fewer moderate and hard sessions [MON-59, MON-60].

### Check-ins (subjective wellness)
- Weight the athlete's self-report above device markers when they disagree. Subjective well-being tracks acute and chronic load more sensitively and consistently than objective markers, and the two often do not correlate. [MON-18]
- Use a short daily check-in covering motivation, physical symptoms or pain, non-training stress, fatigue, physical recovery, general health and feeling in shape. Have each item rated relative to the athlete's normal (worse, normal, better), which needs no long baseline. [MON-19, MON-20]
- Keep the daily check-in brief. Optionally add a longer weekly questionnaire, and use objective tests (for example a standard workout or time trial) about once per training block. [MON-21]

### Wearable and Strava data: what to trust
- Heart rate: wrist optical HR averages about plus/minus 3% error, but error grows with skin tone, intensity, activity type and device. It underestimates HR more in cycling than in walking or treadmill running. For HR-based decisions (zones, drift, intervals), prefer a chest strap, and discount wrist-HR spikes or dropouts. [MON-22, MON-23]
- Watch VO2max: estimates from resting data overestimate (bias +2.17 ml/kg/min, limits of agreement -13.07 to +17.41). Exercise-based estimates are unbiased on average, but an individual reading can be off by about 10 ml/kg/min either way. Use only the within-person trend, never as a lab value or for zone setting. [MON-27]
- Calories: wearable energy expenditure has errors from about -21% to +15%. Treat it as rough and do not base fuelling or weight decisions on it (refer such questions to NUT rules). A meta-analysis found accuracy varied by activity type and that devices combining heart rate with accelerometry had lower error in most activities [MON-25, MON-62].
- Sleep: consumer trackers overestimate total sleep time (error typically over 10%). Use them for trends and habits, not for absolute sleep duration or sleep stages. [MON-26]

### Recovery methods
- For soreness and perceived fatigue between hard sessions, the options with evidence are massage (the most effective for soreness and fatigue), compression garments, water immersion, contrast water, active recovery and cryotherapy. Their effects are on soreness and markers, not on long-term adaptation. [MON-28]
- If an athlete insists on cold-water immersion, use 10-15 min at 10-15 degrees C (or 5-10 degrees C); the evidence is low to very low certainty and the umbrella review favours using it in a planned, context-specific way rather than after every session [MON-63, MON-65].
- Present foam rolling as optional: post-exercise rolling has small effects on muscle pain (g = 0.47) and on loss of sprint and strength performance. [MON-29]
- Advise strength and hypertrophy athletes not to use cold-water immersion right after resistance sessions whose goal is muscle growth (comparative SMD -0.22). Reserve cold water for situations where short-term recovery matters more than adaptation, such as tournaments or races on consecutive days. An umbrella review also found it impaired strength and hypertrophy gains [MON-30, MON-64].

### Sleep
- Adults: aim for 7 or more hours per night regularly. [MON-31]
- Children aged 6-12: 9-12 hours. Teenagers aged 13-18: 8-10 hours per 24 hours. [MON-32]
- Individualise athletes' sleep targets using their perceived sleep need, rather than a fixed 7-9 h. [MON-33]
- If night sleep is short, suggest a nap of under 30 minutes between 13:00 and 16:00. [MON-34]
- Before a key event, extending sleep for a few nights (for example about 8.4 h instead of about 6.8 h) is low-risk and may help endurance performance. The evidence is weak, so offer it as an option. [MON-35]
- After a night of 6 h or less, expect about 7.6% lower performance, and about 0.4% less per hour awake before the session. Move hard or skill-critical sessions earlier in the day, or swap them for an easy session. Morning sessions were largely unaffected. [MON-37, MON-38]

### Injury prevention
- Include strength training in every plan as injury prevention: it cut sports injuries to about one third (RR 0.32-0.34), with a dose-response (each 10% increase in strength-training volume reduced risk by more than 4 percentage points). Balance and proprioception work also helps (RR 0.55). [MON-39, MON-40]
- Do not prescribe stretching for injury prevention (RR 0.96, no effect); use it for mobility goals only. [MON-39]
- For athletes who sprint or play field sports, and for runners doing speed work, include the Nordic hamstring exercise; programmes with it roughly halve hamstring injuries (RR 0.49). [MON-41]
- For endurance runners, be modest: exercise-based prevention programmes did not significantly reduce running injuries overall, and only supervised programmes showed a benefit. Recommend strength work for its other benefits, and use the session-spike rule above for load. [MON-42, MON-11]
- Footwear: no shoe type has been shown to reduce running injuries (very low to low certainty), and prescribing shoes by static foot posture does not reduce injuries (moderate certainty, military recruits). Let the athlete choose a shoe that feels comfortable, and change shoe type gradually. The comfort and gradual-change advice is expert practice, not trial evidence. [MON-43]

### Return to training after a respiratory illness
- Do not rely on the "neck check" rule; use the IOC stepwise process. [MON-44]
- Step one, daily checklist before any training: if the athlete has any symptom at rest (fever, chills, excessive fatigue, body aches, breathing difficulty, chest pain or tightness, dizziness, palpitations, moderate to severe cough, or "not feeling well enough"), no training. [MON-45]
- Step two, if the checklist is clear (mild or no symptoms, no risk conditions): a test session at 60-70% of normal intensity for 10-20 minutes, monitoring symptoms during the session, right after it and 24 hours later. [MON-46]
- Step three, progress frequency, intensity and duration step by step while repeating the symptom check after each session; stop and consult a doctor if the warning symptoms return. [MON-47, MON-48]

## Red flags and referral triggers

- Chest pain, pressure or tightness; palpitations or a racing heart; dizziness; or disproportionate breathlessness during or after the first sessions back after an infection: stop and refer to a doctor. Myocarditis is rare (under 3% after SARS-CoV-2 in larger studies) but is the risk being screened for. [MON-47, MON-50]
- Fever, systemic symptoms or a moderate or severe infection: the return to sport belongs to a physician, not the coach. [MON-45, MON-49]
- Persistent sleep complaints suggesting a sleep disorder (for example insomnia or excessive daytime sleepiness despite enough time in bed): refer to a sleep specialist, because sleep-hygiene advice will not work while a disorder is untreated. [MON-36]
- Pain that changes running or lifting mechanics, or symptoms persisting across check-ins, fall outside load monitoring; follow SAF referral rules (diagnosis and injury treatment are out of scope).

## Contested areas

### ACWR as an injury predictor
- Position A: the 2016 IOC consensus (citing Gabbett and Hulin) recommended keeping weekly increases below 10% or the ACWR within 0.8-1.3. A 2026 multilevel meta-analysis found a small-to-moderate association (g = 0.35). A systematic review of 22 team-sport studies supported the association with non-contact injury, as one tool in a multifaceted system. [MON-06, MON-09, MON-52]
- Position B: methodologists show the ratio is mathematically flawed (coupling, failure to normalise), that simulated data reproduce the association without any causal link, and that load metrics cannot quantify injury risk. The same 2026 meta-analysis reported I2 = 95.8% and concluded the ACWR is not a stand-alone predictive model. In a large runner cohort, ACWR and week-to-week change did not identify risk, while single-session spikes did. A second systematic review found 14 different binning methods and 95% use of the coupled calculation, and a 2020 commentary argues no evidence supports using load data quantitatively to prevent injury. [MON-07, MON-08, MON-09, MON-10, MON-11, MON-12, MON-53, MON-54, MON-55, MON-56]
- Why they differ: observational team-sport data, many arbitrary analysis choices, coupled ratios and confounding by the training schedule.
- What the agent should do: never compute or quote ACWR thresholds as risk numbers. Monitor raw acute and chronic load, avoid sudden large spikes relative to recent history, and progress gradually based on the athlete's response.
- How to explain it to an athlete: "Big sudden jumps are a known risk factor, but no formula can tell you your personal injury risk from your training numbers. We build up steadily and listen to how your body responds."

### The weekly 10% rule
- Position A: the IOC consensus advised keeping weekly increases below 10%. [MON-06]
- Position B: an RCT of the 10% rule in novice runners showed no reduction in injuries, and the large Garmin cohort pointed to single-session spikes rather than weekly change. [MON-13, MON-11, MON-12]
- What the agent should do: use gradual progression as a principle, check single-session spikes for runners, and never promise that 10% steps prevent injury. [MON-06, MON-13, MON-11, MON-12]

### HRV-guided training
- Position A: HRV-guided training better preserves vagal HRV and may produce fewer negative responders; a 2020 meta-analysis found a small significant VO2max advantage (effect size 0.187). [MON-15, MON-16, MON-61]
- Position B: the group-level advantage for fitness and performance is small and not significant (a wearable-HRV meta-analysis: performance g 0.079, VO2peak g 0.171), and HRV-guided plans often simply contained fewer hard sessions [MON-59, MON-60]; protocols (position, index, averaging, band) differ and none is established as best. Wearable "readiness" scores run ahead of this evidence. [MON-15, MON-16, MON-14]
- What the agent should do: offer HRV guidance only to athletes who measure consistently, use 7-day trends together with check-ins [MON-14, MON-24], and never override clear subjective signals with an HRV score [MON-16, MON-17].

### Exercise-based injury prevention in runners versus other sports
- Position A: across sports, strength training greatly reduces injuries (RR about 0.32-0.34), and the Nordic hamstring exercise halves hamstring injuries. [MON-39, MON-40, MON-41]
- Position B: in endurance runners, pooled RCTs show no significant reduction unless the programme is supervised. [MON-42]
- What the agent should do: keep strength work in runners' plans (it is low risk and has performance and general-health value), but do not promise injury prevention, and encourage adherence or supervision when possible.

### Cold-water immersion for recovery
- Position A: it reduces soreness and helps short-term recovery of power and jump performance; a protocol of 10-15 min at 10-15 degrees C appeared most effective [MON-28, MON-63].
- Position B: it impairs strength and hypertrophy gains with chronic use, and the certainty of evidence is low to very low [MON-30, MON-64, MON-65].
- What the agent should do: do not recommend it routinely; avoid it after sessions aimed at muscle growth or strength; reserve it for tournaments or races on consecutive days, using the protocol above.
- How to explain: "Cold water can make you feel fresher tomorrow but may dull the training effect if you use it after every hard session; the research is weak either way."

### The "neck check" rule after illness
- Position A: traditional expert-opinion rule (symptoms "above the neck" allow training). [MON-44]
- Position B: the IOC consensus notes it has little research behind it and replaces it with a symptom checklist, a graded test session and medical review for moderate or severe illness. [MON-44, MON-45, MON-46, MON-49]
- What the agent should do: use the IOC checklist process.

## Evidence gaps

- No validated threshold exists for any load metric (session-RPE, monotony, strain, ACWR, TSS) to predict injury or illness in individual recreational athletes.
- Session-RPE validity is weakest for resistance training and climbing; no climbing-specific load-monitoring evidence was reviewed.
- The best HRV protocol (position, index, averaging window, decision band) is unknown. Short-recording accuracy of portable devices is acceptable on average (MON-57), but the accuracy of overnight HRV and proprietary "readiness" scores from current consumer devices is not established here.
- Evidence on sleep extension, banking sleep and napping in athletes comes from small, poorly controlled studies.
- Counting rest days as zero load in the monotony calculation is a common convention that no saved source states; it is not recorded as a rule.
- How long respiratory illnesses typically last in athletes was not recorded from a source, so no expected recovery time is given.
- Evidence for preventing running injuries is weak: no shoe type, programme or progression rule has strong RCT support. The single-session spike rule comes from one observational cohort.
- Return to training after illness rests mainly on expert consensus; the number of days to return comes from few studies (0 to about 8 days).
- The age range in youth sleep recommendations and the injury-prevention trials (12-40 years) leaves older masters athletes under-represented.
