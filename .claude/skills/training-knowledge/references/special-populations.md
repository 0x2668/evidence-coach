# Physical activity dose for health and special populations

Evidence levels: consensus, meta-analysis, single study, expert opinion. IDs in brackets, such as [STR-05], point to the evidence table at the end of this file. Generated from the verified research notes on 2026-10-30; do not edit by hand.

## Practical rules

### Weekly dose for healthy adults (18-64)
- Default health target for adults: 150-300 min/week of moderate-intensity or 75-150 min/week of vigorous-intensity aerobic activity, or an equivalent combination [POP-01]. This is a strong recommendation that also covers adults with chronic conditions or disability, as able [POP-01, POP-11].
- Add muscle-strengthening work at moderate or greater intensity for all major muscle groups on 2 or more days per week [POP-02].
- ACSM alternative framing for apparently healthy adults: moderate exercise at least 30 min/day on at least 5 days (at least 150 min/week), or vigorous exercise at least 20 min/day on at least 3 days (at least 75 min/week), or a combination [POP-19]. Also schedule resistance and neuromotor (balance, agility, coordination) exercise on 2-3 days/week, and flexibility work for each major muscle-tendon group at 60 s per exercise on at least 2 days/week [POP-20]. The ACSM statement dates from 2011; the WHO numbers are the primary default [POP-19].
- Treat these doses as minimums for health, not as a ceiling or a plan for a competitive athlete [POP-12].
- Tell clients that some activity is better than none, and that people below the recommendation still benefit [POP-03].
- Limit sedentary time and replace it with activity of any intensity; very sedentary adults should aim above the minimum moderate-to-vigorous target. Do not quote a numeric sitting limit, because WHO found insufficient evidence to set one [POP-06, POP-07].

### Starting from sedentary
- Clear the client through the SAF screening first. After a clear PAR-Q+ (no to all questions), start with 20-60 min of low-to-moderate exercise on 3-5 days/week that includes aerobic and muscle-strengthening work, then progress toward 150 min/week or more of moderate activity [POP-65].
- Medical clearance is generally not needed before light or moderate activity (up to brisk walking) in people without contraindications [POP-04]. Medical consultation and exercise testing are for clinical indications, not universal screening. Teach the client the symptoms of coronary disease and progress intensity and volume gradually [POP-21].
- Progress in this order: increase duration and frequency before intensity. Begin at relatively moderate intensity, or light if fitness is low, and avoid relatively vigorous activity such as running at first [POP-13, POP-15].
- Use short sessions spread through the week and work gradually toward 150 min/week [POP-13]. Adding 5-15 minutes of walking per session, 2-3 times per week, to usual activity carries a low risk of musculoskeletal injury and no known risk of severe cardiac events [POP-17].
- Judge each increase by its relative size: +20 min is 10% for someone doing 200 min/week but 50% for someone doing 40 min/week. This is an illustration of why small baselines need smaller steps, not a validated 10% rule [POP-16].
- Step interval: young people can probably increase in small steps every 1-2 weeks; older adults appear to need 2-4 weeks to adapt to each new level [POP-18]. Where an adult falls between these groups, pick the slower step and watch recovery (coach judgement; no claim defines the age cut-off, see Gaps).
- Strength for inactive adults: start with 1 day/week at light-to-moderate effort, move to 2 days and possibly more, and raise the intensity slightly each week until it is moderate or greater [POP-14, POP-02].
- Vigorous work: people already doing regular moderate activity can progress gradually to vigorous activity without consulting a clinician [POP-05]. Adults over 45 who are not accustomed to vigorous or maximal efforts should consult a qualified exercise professional before those efforts [POP-65]. Anyone who develops new symptoms while increasing activity should see a healthcare provider [POP-05].
- Test scenario, a 45-year-old after years of inactivity with 3 x 45 min per week: after a clear PAR-Q+, the plan fits the 20-60 min and 3-5 days/week starting range [POP-65]. Make every session a mix of aerobic and muscle-strengthening work at low-to-moderate effort, not a run or a hard class [POP-65, POP-15]. Within each 45 min, build the aerobic part in short, easy blocks and let duration grow before intensity [POP-13, POP-15]. Begin strength at 1 day/week of light work, going to 2 days over the following weeks [POP-14]. Treat three such sessions as a base that is close to 150 min/week; add short walks on other days to reach and then pass it [POP-13, POP-17]. A 45-year-old is at the edge of the "over 45" advice, so also apply the rule against vigorous or maximal efforts until the client is accustomed to them [POP-65]. Stop and refer for any new symptom [POP-05, POP-62].

### Older adults (65+)
- Weekly dose: within the usual weekly activity, include varied multicomponent activity that emphasises functional balance and strength training at moderate or greater intensity on 3 or more days per week, to prevent falls [POP-08].
- Falls-prevention exercise: balance-challenging and functional exercise (for example sit-to-stand and stepping), three or more sessions weekly, individualised, progressed for at least 12 weeks and continued longer [POP-27].
- Always include balance work. Exercise reduces the rate of falls by 23% in community-dwelling older people (high certainty), and multicomponent programmes (balance and functional plus resistance) probably reduce it by 34%. Programmes that are mainly resistance training, dance or walking have uncertain effects on falls [POP-32, POP-33, POP-34].
- Resistance training: 2-3 times per week, starting with 1-2 sets and progressing to 2-3 sets of 8-12 repetitions for the major muscle groups used in daily function [POP-22]. Start at 30-40% of 1RM and progress to 70-80% of 1RM [POP-23].
- Endurance sessions can start at 5-10 min in the first weeks and progress to 15-30 min, 3-7 days per week [POP-26].
- Progress each new level over 2-4 weeks, because older adults appear to need that long to adapt [POP-18].
- Frailty is not a contraindication to resistance exercise; it is a key reason to prescribe it, starting with low absolute loads. Train frail clients with clinical oversight or qualified staff [POP-24].
- Consensus support: the NSCA position statement calls resistance training a powerful intervention against loss of strength and muscle mass and their effects on function, mobility and independence in older adults. Use it to justify including strength work; take the dose numbers from the rules above [POP-79, POP-22].
- Older adults with disability may need more direct supervision. Be cautious in the first phase, because even a small injury can take weeks to recover from [POP-25].
- Intermediate falls risk (one non-severe fall plus gait or balance problems): strength and balance training is the indicated intervention [POP-29]. High falls risk goes to referral, see below [POP-28, POP-30].

### Adolescents
- Weekly dose (children and adolescents 5-17): average at least 60 min/day of mostly aerobic moderate-to-vigorous activity across the week, with vigorous aerobic and muscle- and bone-strengthening activities on at least 3 days per week [POP-09].
- Resistance training is safe and effective for adolescents when qualified professionals supervise the sessions and teach proper technique [POP-47]. No growth-plate injury has been reported in supervised prospective youth studies, and there is no evidence that resistance training stunts growth [POP-48].
- Programme focus is learning technique, not the load lifted [POP-49]. Never run adolescent lifting unsupervised.
- Youth resistance-training dose: start at 1-2 sets at no more than 60% 1RM; once technique is competent, progress to about 2-4 sets of 6-12 repetitions at up to 80% 1RM; only with more training age and sound technique add periodic phases of 6 or fewer repetitions above 85% 1RM [POP-81, POP-82, POP-83].
- Train youth 2-3 times per week on non-consecutive days, with about 1 min rest between sets, longer (2-3 min) for heavier or more technical work [POP-84, POP-85].
- Current consensus bodies agree that youth resistance training is worthwhile when appropriately prescribed and supervised: the NSCA (2009) and the American Academy of Pediatrics (2020, which names nonathletes as well as athletes). The NSCA adds that youth programmes follow age-specific guidelines, so do not copy an adult programme [POP-75, POP-76, POP-78]. Both sources were read as abstracts only, so they give no teen-specific loads, sets or frequency.
- In US emergency-room records most youth lifting injuries were accidents (dropped weights, improper equipment use) that supervision and stricter safety rules could prevent. Keep equipment safe, spot and supervise every session [POP-77].
- Test scenario, a teenage athlete who wants to bulk. First, agree supervised resistance training with a qualified professional who teaches technique, with load progression secondary to technique [POP-47, POP-49]. Second, reassure on growth, since supervised training does not stunt it [POP-48]. Third, treat a "bulking" goal as a prompt to ask about body image and appearance motives, because adolescents use performance-enhancing substances for appearance as well as performance [POP-50]. Fourth, do not supply, recommend or discuss dosing of anabolic steroids, prohormones or other performance-enhancing drugs, and do not recommend supplements to a minor; for most young athletes these add little beyond puberty plus sound training and nutrition [POP-52, POP-51]. Fifth, if the athlete or parents ask about supplements such as creatine, refer to a physician or sports dietitian with parental involvement [POP-53]. Safeguarding of minors (parental consent, second adult present, reporting duties) is not covered by these claims; follow the employer's or club's own safeguarding policy (see Gaps).

### Pregnancy
- Without contraindications, pregnant women should accumulate at least 150 min/week of moderate-intensity activity [POP-35]. Without obstetric or medical complications, physical activity in pregnancy is safe and desirable, and women should be encouraged to continue or start safe activities [POP-40].
- Recommend daily pelvic floor muscle training with technique instruction [POP-36].
- Pregnant athletes, or women training well above the guideline, should be supervised by a specialist healthcare provider [POP-10].
- Obtain the obstetric provider's agreement before programming. With an absolute contraindication only usual daily activities are allowed, no strenuous exercise; with a relative contraindication, discuss moderate-to-vigorous activity with the obstetric care provider before taking part [POP-37].
- Avoid activities with contact or abdominal-impact risk, fall-prone activities (downhill skiing, surfing, off-road cycling, gymnastics, riding), hot yoga or Pilates, scuba diving, skydiving, and activity above 6,000 ft unless the woman lives at altitude [POP-55]. For cyclists, trail runners and climbers, ask the obstetric provider about fall risk before continuing those activities [POP-55].

### Postpartum
- First year postpartum, without contraindications: at least 120 min/week of moderate-to-vigorous activity over four or more days, mixing aerobic and resistance training [POP-41].
- Start with light activity (gentle walking, pelvic floor training) and progress to moderate-to-vigorous activity once incisions or perineal tears have healed and bleeding does not increase with that activity [POP-43].
- Individualise progression; it is gradual and symptom-based, with no fixed week-by-week timeline [POP-44].
- Daily pelvic floor muscle training, with technique instruction from a pelvic floor physiotherapist [POP-42].
- Mental health, pelvic floor and abdominal wall function, musculoskeletal pain and wound healing are not contraindications, but may need screening or treatment by a healthcare provider or qualified exercise professional [POP-46].

### Chronic conditions: what each requires
- General: adults with chronic conditions aim for the guideline as able, and may need a healthcare professional's advice on types and amounts of activity given complications, medications and treatment plan [POP-11].
- Type 2 diabetes, clearance: no medical evaluation is needed before low-to-moderate activity such as brisk walking unless cardiovascular symptoms or microvascular complications exist. Sedentary adults with type 2 diabetes need medical clearance before moderate-to-high-intensity activity [POP-56].
- Type 2 diabetes, hypoglycaemia: risk is minimal with lifestyle management alone but raised with insulin and insulin secretagogues (sulfonylureas, meglitinides). Clients on insulin or sulfonylureas should carry rapid-acting carbohydrate during activity. Medication dose changes are for the treating clinician, not the coach [POP-57, POP-58].
- Type 2 diabetes, glucose: do not start exercise with blood glucose above 250 mg/dL (13.9 mmol/L) if ketones are moderate or high; above 300 mg/dL (16.7 mmol/L) without ketones, exercise only with caution, hydrated and feeling well [POP-59].
- Diabetes with retinopathy: exercise is contraindicated with unstable or untreated proliferative retinopathy or after recent eye surgery or laser treatment; with severe retinopathy avoid breath-holding, heavy lifting and head-down positions [POP-60].
- Hypertension: with controlled hypertension, low-to-moderate-intensity resistance training with proper breathing is safe; people on antihypertensives should use extended cool-downs to prevent hypotension [POP-61]. Resting blood pressure of 160/90 mmHg or higher (or unknown) in someone with hypertension needs further evaluation before becoming more active [POP-64].
- Diagnosed cardiovascular disease: use low-to-moderate resistance training with proper breathing only after clearance [POP-61, POP-62]. Dizziness, excessive breathlessness, chest pain or pressure, or palpitations mean stop and send for immediate medical evaluation; training resumes only after clearance [POP-62]. People with pacemakers or implanted defibrillators consult their physician before upper-body resistance training [POP-63].
- Osteoarthritis: tailored therapeutic exercise (local strengthening plus general aerobic fitness) is a core treatment for everyone with osteoarthritis [POP-71]. Warn clients that joint pain may rise when they start, and that regular exercise still benefits their joints [POP-72].
- The EULAR 2025 update says physical activity promotion should be standard care for people with osteoarthritis and inflammatory arthritis; its dose details were not available, so keep to the NICE direction above [POP-80, POP-71].
- Low back pain: encourage continuing normal activities. Exercise programmes (strengthening, aerobic, mind-body or combined) are recommended, chosen by the person's preference and capability [POP-74]. Specific causes must be excluded by a clinician first, and new or changed symptoms need clinical review [POP-73].
- Osteoporosis, safety: exercise is unlikely to cause fractures and there is little evidence of harm [POP-66].
- Osteoporosis, resistance: progressive resistance training on two or three days per week, building to loads that can be lifted 8-12 times and up to three sets, after learning technique at lower intensity [POP-67].
- Osteoporosis, impact: without vertebral or multiple low-trauma fractures, use moderate impact (stamping, jogging, low jumps, hops) on most days, at least 50 impacts per session [POP-68]. With vertebral or multiple low-trauma fractures, keep impact up to brisk walking (aiming for 150 minutes over the week) and obtain individualised physiotherapist advice for impact and resistance training [POP-69].
- Osteoporosis, spine: modify or avoid sustained, repeated or end-range spinal flexion and loaded excessive back rounding; teach a hip hinge instead of banning lifting [POP-70].

## Red flags and referral triggers

- Pregnancy, stop signs: vaginal bleeding, dizziness or faintness, shortness of breath before exercise, chest pain, headache, muscle weakness, calf pain or swelling, regular painful contractions, fluid leaking from the vagina. Stop exercise and tell the client to contact the obstetric provider [POP-54].
- Pregnancy, do not train without obstetric clearance: any absolute contraindication (ruptured membranes, premature labour, unexplained persistent vaginal bleeding, placenta previa after 28 weeks, preeclampsia, incompetent cervix, growth restriction, triplets or more, uncontrolled type 1 diabetes, hypertension or thyroid disease, other serious cardiovascular, respiratory or systemic disorders) limits her to usual daily activities [POP-37, POP-38]. Relative contraindications (recurrent pregnancy loss, gestational hypertension, previous spontaneous preterm birth, mild or moderate cardiovascular or respiratory disease, symptomatic anaemia, malnutrition, eating disorder, twins after week 28) need a discussion with the obstetric care provider first [POP-37, POP-39].
- Postpartum, relative contraindications that need a healthcare provider before moderate-to-vigorous activity: severe abdominal pain, non-menstrual vaginal bleeding, postpartum cardiomyopathy, caesarean symptoms worsening with activity, unstable blood pressure above 140/90 mmHg, and also eating disorder, malnutrition, possible anaemia or low energy availability, signs of deep vein thrombosis, chest pain, dizziness, syncope [POP-45]. Increased bleeding with activity means step back to light activity [POP-43].
- Older adults, high falls risk: a fall with injury needing medical treatment, 2 or more falls in the previous 12 months, lying on the floor unable to get up for at least an hour, frailty, or suspected transient loss of consciousness. Refer for a multifactorial falls risk assessment by clinicians, and for syncope evaluation if a fall was suspected to be syncopal [POP-28, POP-30].
- Older adults, frailty screen: the frailty phenotype has 5 criteria (slow gait, low activity, unintentional weight loss, exhaustion, weakness); 3 or more means frail and 1 or 2 means prefrail. Frail clients are trained with clinical oversight [POP-31, POP-24].
- Older adults, new fall plus gait or balance problems: intermediate risk, so start strength and balance training [POP-29]; any worsening goes back to clinical review [POP-28].
- Blood pressure: in a client with hypertension, resting blood pressure of 160/90 mmHg or higher (or unknown) triggers further evaluation by a qualified professional before they become more active [POP-64]. This is a screening threshold, not a treatment target [POP-64].
- Cardiovascular disease: dizziness, excessive breathlessness, chest pain or pressure, or palpitations during training mean stop and immediate medical evaluation; training resumes only after further clearance. Implanted cardiac device: physician consultation before upper-body resistance work [POP-62, POP-63].
- Adults without known disease: new symptoms while increasing activity mean see a healthcare provider [POP-05]. Adults over 45 not accustomed to vigorous exercise consult a qualified exercise professional before vigorous or maximal efforts [POP-65].
- Type 2 diabetes: medical clearance for sedentary adults before moderate-to-high-intensity activity, or before any activity if cardiovascular symptoms or microvascular complications exist [POP-56]. Insulin or sulfonylurea users: hypoglycaemia risk, carbohydrate on hand, and any medication change is referred to the treating clinician [POP-57, POP-58]. High glucose with ketones: do not start [POP-59]. Proliferative retinopathy or recent eye surgery or laser: exercise contraindicated until cleared [POP-60].
- Osteoporosis with vertebral or multiple low-trauma fractures: physiotherapist advice before impact or resistance progression [POP-69].
- Low back pain: new or changed symptoms go to clinical review; the coach does not diagnose the cause [POP-73].
- Adolescents: any interest in steroids, prohormones or other performance-enhancing drugs, or signs that body-image concerns drive a bulking goal, go to a physician with parental involvement [POP-50, POP-53].
- Frail or disabled older adults: more direct supervision and clinical oversight in the first phase [POP-24, POP-25].

## Contested areas

### Supplements and performance-enhancing substances in minors
- Position A (AAP clinical report): legal supplements in youth raise concerns of frequent contamination, association with later anabolic steroid use, and harm to the youth sport experience; for most young athletes supplements add little beyond puberty plus sound training and nutrition [POP-51, POP-52].
- Position B (ISSN position stand): creatine may be considered for younger athletes only if they are in serious supervised training, eat a well-balanced diet, are informed about proper use and do not exceed recommended doses [POP-53].
- Why they differ: the AAP weighs contamination, gateway behaviour and body-image risks across the whole youth population; ISSN focuses on creatine's safety record in monitored athletes, and its authors have industry ties (source note). The AAP report was accessible only as an abstract.
- What the agent should do: do not recommend supplements to minors; this is outside a coach's scope. Put training, sleep and food first, and refer questions to a physician or sports dietitian with parental involvement [POP-52, POP-53].
- How to explain it: "For most teenagers, the big gains come from growing up, regular training and enough food. Experts disagree about whether creatine is acceptable, and that decision belongs with a doctor or dietitian and your parents."

## Evidence gaps

- Safeguarding of minors (consent, chaperoning, reporting) was not researched; the agent should defer to the employer's or club's policy and flag this to the human trainer.
- No claim gives the aerobic dose for adults 65+ with a number (POP-08 covers multicomponent training only; POP-26 covers session length for endurance work). The agent should not invent a separate weekly aerobic figure for older adults.
- No claim defines where "older adult" begins for the 1-2 week versus 2-4 week step interval [POP-18], and the 45-year-old sits between the "young" and "older" groups. POP-18 is worded with "probably" and "appear", so certainty is low; the orchestrator may want to flag it as contested.
- Pregnancy: no claims on resistance-training loads, heart-rate or perceived-exertion ceilings, supine positions or heat beyond hot yoga. Postpartum: no claim on diastasis recti or return to running or impact.
- Adolescents: no claim on the energy intake needed for growth; no claim on relative energy deficiency or eating-disorder screening for bulking or cutting teenagers. The newly saved NSCA, AAP, Lloyd and IOC texts are abstracts, so this gap stays open until a full text (for example the NSCA 2009 position points or Lloyd 2014 in BJSM) is saved.
- Chronic conditions not covered: chronic kidney disease, COPD or asthma, cancer, depression, multiple sclerosis, rheumatoid arthritis, fibromyalgia, and heart failure. Cardiovascular disease has no numeric exercise doses in the claims.
- Hypertension: no claims on how to measure resting blood pressure, on exercise blood-pressure stopping limits, or on target aerobic doses.
- Osteoarthritis and low back pain: no dose numbers (frequency, load, duration) in the claims; the agent has direction only. Low back pain: no claim on acute versus chronic stages or on red-flag symptom lists beyond the causes named in POP-73.
- Osteoporosis impact advice for those with vertebral fractures is based on theoretical, unproved risk and consensus grading (source note, POP-69); the orchestrator may want to flag it as low certainty.
- Climbing, trail running and ultra-endurance in special populations (for example pregnant climbers, older trail runners) have no direct evidence here; only fall-prone activity cautions in POP-55 apply.
