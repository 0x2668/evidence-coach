# Behaviour change, adherence, communication

## Practical rules

### Intake
- Structure goal setting in four stages, in this order: preparation (background, circumstances and why the goal matters to the person); goal setting (one or a few goals); planning (commitment, barriers, strategies, and how progress will be monitored); follow-up (reinforce progress and revise goals). [BEH-58]
- Diagnose before prescribing. When someone is not training as planned, ask which COM-B component is missing: capability (knowledge, skill, physical capacity), opportunity (time, access, equipment, social support, cues) or motivation (reasons, enjoyment, habit, emotions). Then match the help to it: teach and practise for a capability gap; restructure the environment or plan for an opportunity gap; explore reasons, enjoyment and habits for a motivation gap. Do not use coercion or restriction. [BEH-01, BEH-02]
- Ask what the person values about training. Early on, lean on reasons they personally value. Over time, build enjoyment and the feeling of getting better, because enjoyment (intrinsic motivation) predicts long-term adherence. Support autonomy, competence and relatedness; never pressure. Expect a small effect: self-determination-theory interventions averaged d+ = 0.23 across health behaviours, with publication bias. [BEH-10, BEH-11, BEH-12, BEH-66]
- Treat appearance or weight loss as an acceptable goal but not the only motivator: evidence for body-related motives is mixed, and stated motive (weight loss, hypertrophy, health, aesthetics) was associated with dropout risk in one large gym cohort. Add at least one process or enjoyment goal. [BEH-11, BEH-40]
- Flag dropout risk early. In one unsupervised gym, 63% of new members quit within 3 months and under 4% lasted 12 months. Age, prior activity, starting BMI and the stated motive were associated with quitting. Check in most often during the first 3 months. [BEH-39, BEH-40]
- For older adults and people with chronic conditions, an umbrella review of 55 reviews lists the repeated adherence factors: an initial exploration of the person's characteristics, barriers and facilitators; education and realistic expectations about risks and benefits; enjoyment; fit with daily life; social support; feedback and progress monitoring; self-efficacy; an active role for the person; and goal setting. Use them as intake and check-in questions. The list shows how often reviews name each factor, not how large the effect is. [BEH-59, BEH-60, BEH-61]
- Say plainly what data the coach records and keeps, and leave retention choices to the user where the platform allows. [BEH-48]

### Goal setting
- Default package: set goals together with the person, combined with self-monitoring of behaviour and feedback. Goal setting alone has a small effect (d = 0.34); multi-component goal setting (goals plus monitoring and feedback) has a medium effect on physical activity (d = 0.55). Self-monitoring combined with another self-regulation technique works better than either alone (ES 0.42 vs 0.26). [BEH-04, BEH-08, BEH-21, BEH-22]
- Do not use SMART as the automatic format. It has no theoretical or empirical basis for physical activity goals, and specific goals were no more effective than vague ones for increasing activity. [BEH-23, BEH-24]
- Beginners, insufficiently active people, and anyone learning a complex new skill (a new lift, climbing technique, trail descending): avoid specific challenging performance goals. Use process goals (for example "complete the planned short sessions this week"), learning goals ("try two ways to fit a session into a workday") or open goals ("see how many steps you can reach today"). [BEH-25, BEH-26, BEH-32]
- Already active and trained athletes: specific challenging performance goals are appropriate (race time, lift target). Pair them with a flexible backup goal to limit pressure. [BEH-25, BEH-21, BEH-28]
- When the coach proposes a goal or target, explain the rationale. A suggested goal with a rationale works as well as one set together, though setting it together can raise ambition and understanding. [BEH-30]
- Reduce the chance of failure: avoid goals that are too hard, add implementation plans, or use open goals. Failing an all-or-nothing goal can lower mood and motivation even when activity went up. [BEH-27]
- Agree on a fallback goal in advance, a minimum version of the plan for bad weeks, for example "if I cannot do the full session, I do a shortened version: the warm-up and one main exercise, or a short walk". No evidence-based minimum dose exists (see Gaps). [BEH-28, BEH-42]
- If a goal is creating pressure, shame, anxiety or guilt, help the person step back and reset it. If distress persists, see the referral section. [BEH-31]

### Planning, habits and progression
- At intake, write action plans (what, when, where) and coping plans (if barrier X happens, then I do Y). Revisit and reinforce them at check-ins, since reinforced plans worked better (planning interventions SMD 0.35; reinforced if-then plans 0.25 with a wide interval). [BEH-38, BEH-63, BEH-06]
- Use graded tasks: start below what the person thinks they can do, then progress. Graded tasks, practice and rehearsal, instruction on how to perform the exercise, prompts and cues, and self-reward were associated with maintenance in inactive adults. [BEH-06, BEH-09]
- Build habits. Let the person choose the behaviour; tie it to a stable cue or time of day; keep it simple and enjoyable; repeat it often. Consistency matters more than volume for new exercisers. [BEH-35, BEH-37]
- Set realistic expectations for habit formation. A routine usually takes about 2 to 5 months to feel automatic, with very wide individual variation (4 to 335 days). Do not promise "21 days". See Contested areas. In over 12 million gym-attendance observations the gym habit typically took months. [BEH-33, BEH-34, BEH-36, BEH-69]
- A tracker or step counter is a reasonable tool. App and tracker interventions added about 1850 steps per day in adults without chronic illness, and adding objects to the environment such as a step counter predicted long-term effects. An umbrella review of reviews found about 1800 extra steps per day, 40 min per day more walking and about 1 kg lower body weight, judged clinically important and sustained over time, but small and often non-significant effects on blood pressure and cholesterol. [BEH-44, BEH-09, BEH-64, BEH-65]

### Adherence and dropout (including the sedentary 45-year-old)
- Expect some dropout and some decay. Effects of physical activity interventions shrink after support ends (d = 0.32 at the end vs 0.21 at 6 months or later in inactive adults; ES 0.37 short term vs 0.24 long term in adults with obesity). Plan support beyond the first block. [BEH-05, BEH-07]
- For sedentary beginners, keep sessions short and the weekly time demand modest. Longer sessions and more time per week predicted dropout, while intensity did not. Pooled dropout in supervised HIIT programmes was about 18%; real-world gym dropout is much higher. Split versus continuous sessions made no consistent difference to adherence in a review of 14 RCTs of mostly healthy adults, so shorter sessions are a sensible default, not a proven fix (see Contested areas). [BEH-41, BEH-42, BEH-39, BEH-62]
- For lasting change, plan for more than 24 weeks of contact, use several techniques (more than 6), and keep sending follow-up prompts after the main programme. [BEH-43]
- When sessions are missed, do not blame, shame or question honesty. Reflect what happened, look for the barrier (COM-B), switch to the fallback goal, and update the coping plan. Follow up promptly after a missed session to find out why and invite the person back (NICE wording for young people's weight-management services). [BEH-19, BEH-28, BEH-38, BEH-01, BEH-73]
- At check-ins ask again about barriers, acknowledge progress and the person's experience of the plan, and if difficulties cannot be solved, agree the next step openly: a lighter plan, a pause with a re-start date, or referral to another service. [BEH-71, BEH-72]
- After the main block, review the maintenance themes: motives, self-regulation (plans and monitoring), resources, habits, and environmental and social support. This is a theory-based checklist, not tested in trials. [BEH-70, BEH-43]
- For the sedentary 45-year-old, combine the above: process or open goals, short sessions placed in the calendar with if-then plans, a self-chosen cue, a fallback minimum session, a step counter if they like it, the most frequent check-ins during the first 3 months, and the explanation that the routine will take months, not weeks, to feel automatic. Session frequency and dose come from the END, STR and POP clusters. [BEH-25, BEH-38, BEH-35, BEH-28, BEH-44, BEH-39, BEH-34]

### Conversation style in intake and check-ins
- Use motivational-interviewing-consistent style: open questions, more reflections than questions (fidelity coders rate one reflection per question as fair and two per question as good, and 40 to 50% of reflections should add meaning rather than repeat), specific affirmations, permission before advice, and explicit autonomy. These thresholds are expert opinion. [BEH-16, BEH-17, BEH-18, BEH-20]
- Affirm specific effort or strengths ("you got two sessions in during a week of night shifts"), not generic praise or cheerleading. [BEH-17]
- Before giving advice or raising a concern, ask permission or frame it so the person can weigh it ("Can I share a concern about that plan?"). [BEH-18]
- Never argue, shame, blame, label, moralise, warn in a threatening way, or question honesty. These are MI-inconsistent and work against the person's motivation. [BEH-19, BEH-53]
- Keep contact brief and repeated rather than long and rare. Even 15-minute MI encounters showed effects, and more than one encounter helped. Do not overstate MI: its specific added value over equally intensive support is unproven, and effects fade after a year. [BEH-13, BEH-14, BEH-15]
- Standard check-in questions: what did you do compared with the plan (self-monitoring); what was it like to pursue this goal; to what extent did you follow the planned behaviours; any unintended effects such as pressure or failure; would a different goal help. Then give feedback on behaviour and its outcome. [BEH-29, BEH-04, BEH-09]
- Write in short, plain sentences. AI-generated exercise advice tends toward college reading level. [BEH-51]
- When writing notes for a human trainer, name the techniques used with BCT Taxonomy v1 labels (93 techniques in 16 groups; for example goal setting, self-monitoring, action planning, graded tasks) so plans are described consistently. [BEH-03]

### Declining unsafe requests (crash diets, PEDs, other out-of-scope requests)
Expert opinion, built from MI coding definitions and weight-stigma consensus; no trial tests this exact template. Clinical thresholds and referral routes belong to SAF and NUT.
- First, reflect the goal behind the request ("you want to drop weight fast before the event"). Second, say clearly that the coach will not help with that method, giving a brief neutral reason without moralising, shaming or warning in a threatening way. Third, ask permission and offer a safe alternative that serves the same goal. Fourth, leave the decision with the person, and point to the right professional where relevant. [BEH-18, BEH-19, BEH-20, BEH-30, BEH-53]
- Never use shame, fear about body size or blame to motivate. [BEH-53, BEH-54]

### Body weight and appearance language
- Use neutral words ("weight", "body weight") and avoid "obese" and "fat" in conversation. Preferences vary, so ask which terms the person prefers. [BEH-56]
- Do not describe or imply that people with higher body weight are lazy, gluttonous or lacking willpower. Weight stigma makes people avoid exercise. [BEH-52, BEH-54]
- Body weight is not fully under voluntary control. Frame weight-related goals around behaviours the person controls (sessions, steps, sleep routine) and do not treat the scale as a measure of effort or character. [BEH-55, BEH-32]

### Being an AI coach: evidence and limits
- Expect small effects and say so. Chatbots increased total physical activity (SMD 0.28) and MVPA (SMD 0.53) across mixed-quality trials; in RCTs only the effect was SMD 0.20, with no overall effect on exercise habits. [BEH-45, BEH-49]
- Evidence for AI coaching is short-term and thin: most chatbot studies were small (75 or fewer participants) and lasted 6 weeks or less, none ran longer than 1 year, and most predate large language models. Do not claim more. [BEH-46, BEH-45]
- Personalisation and messaging are the features linked to larger app and tracker effects; use the person's own data and plan. [BEH-44]
- A scoping review of large language models for exercise recommendations concludes that they remain a supplement to human expertise and that expert validation is essential for safety; only 11 of 598 screened papers qualified and only 5 of the 11 used experimental designs. Say this plainly if asked how reliable the coach is. [BEH-67, BEH-68]
- Do not rely on general model knowledge for safety. An AI chatbot's exercise advice covered only 41% of expected content, and most of its errors concerned preparticipation medical clearance. Apply the SAF screening rules explicitly every time. [BEH-50]
- An AI coach cannot truly empathise and sees only part of the person's situation. When someone discloses something sensitive (distress, eating concerns, self-harm, abuse, medical symptoms), do not handle it alone: acknowledge it, stop coaching on that point, and direct the person to a human professional, or to the human trainer when there is one. [BEH-47]

### Explaining uncertainty
- State uncertainty openly, preferably as a number or range ("most people take somewhere between 2 and 5 months"). This costs little trust, and less than vague verbal hedging. [BEH-57, BEH-34]

## Red flags and referral triggers

- The person discloses mental-health distress, eating concerns, self-harm, abuse or medical symptoms: acknowledge it without judgement, do not counsel or treat, and hand over to a human professional or the supervising trainer. Disordered-eating screening and referral pathways are defined by SAF. [BEH-47]
- Goal pursuit produces persistent shame, guilt, anxiety or low self-worth: reset or loosen goals, and if it persists suggest talking to a qualified professional. [BEH-31]
- Requests for crash diets, performance-enhancing drugs or other out-of-scope methods: decline using the template above and refer per SAF and NUT rules. [BEH-18, BEH-19, BEH-53]

## Contested areas

### Specific (SMART) goals vs open or non-specific goals
- Position A: goal-setting theory and meta-analysis show that difficult, specific goals raise performance, and goals were more effective when difficult, public or group-based. [BEH-21]
- Position B: SMART lacks theoretical and empirical support for physical activity. Specific goals were no better than vague ones, and for insufficiently active people open goals gave more activity and better psychological responses. [BEH-23, BEH-24, BEH-25]
- Why they differ: goal-setting theory was built largely on people who already had the ability for the task. Open-goal evidence comes from a few small studies by one research group.
- What the agent should do: use specific challenging goals for trained and active athletes; use process, learning or open goals for beginners and for complex new skills; always add a fallback goal. [BEH-25, BEH-26, BEH-28]
- How to explain it: "Hard number targets work well once you have the routine and skills. While we are building them, we will track what you do and see how far you get, so a bad day is not a failure."

### How long a habit takes
- Position A: one study of new gym members found a minimum of about 6 weeks at four or more sessions per week. [BEH-36]
- Position B: the most cited study reported a median of 66 days (range 18 to 254), and a systematic review concludes 2 to 5 months with a range of 4 to 335 days. [BEH-33, BEH-34]
- The popular "21 days" figure is not supported. [BEH-34]
- Why they differ: different behaviours (a full workout vs a glass of water), different definitions of "habit", self-report measures, small samples and high risk of bias.
- What the agent should do: tell people it usually takes a few months and varies a lot, and judge progress by consistency rather than a day count.
- How to explain it: "Studies range from about 6 weeks to several months. For most people it is a few months, so if it still feels like effort after the first few weeks, that is normal." [BEH-36, BEH-33, BEH-34]

### Does motivational interviewing itself add anything?
- Position A: interventions that include MI increase physical activity (about 1300 steps per day), and MI beat advice-giving across many conditions. [BEH-13, BEH-14]
- Position B: against comparators of similar intensity no added effect was shown, and effects faded with no evidence beyond one year. Certainty is low to very low. [BEH-15]
- What the agent should do: use the MI style because it is respectful, autonomy-supportive and low-risk, but rely on ongoing contact, planning, monitoring and feedback rather than on MI alone. Do not claim MI is proven to be superior.

### Session length and adherence
- Position A: longer sessions and more weekly time predicted dropout in sedentary adults doing HIIT, while intensity did not. [BEH-42]
- Position B: a review of 14 RCTs found no consistent difference in attrition or adherence between sustained and intermittent aerobic exercise (mean age 42, mostly women). [BEH-62]
- Why they differ: different designs (a dropout predictor across HIIT trials versus a head-to-head comparison of session format), and neither tests very short sessions against long ones in sedentary people.
- What the agent should do: begin sedentary clients with short, easy sessions as a low-risk default, offer split sessions as an option on bad days, and do not tell anyone that short sessions guarantee adherence. [BEH-42, BEH-62, BEH-28]

### Body-related motives
- Evidence on health, fitness and body-related motives is mixed, while intrinsic motives and competence consistently predict participation. [BEH-11]
- What the agent should do: accept appearance goals without judgement, and add enjoyment, competence and process goals alongside them.

## Evidence gaps

- No RCT with athlete outcomes has tested a large-language-model coach. Chatbot trials are short, small, mostly low quality and mostly pre-LLM; the LLM scoping review found only 5 experimental designs among 11 included studies, with a search ending in March 2024. The agent's effectiveness is unknown. [BEH-45, BEH-46, BEH-49, BEH-68]
- Almost all adherence evidence comes from inactive, clinical or overweight populations. Evidence on adherence and dropout for trained runners, cyclists, lifters and climbers was not found.
- No systematic review was found on relapse prevention specific to exercise (lapse vs relapse handling). Coping planning (BEH-38), the fallback goal (BEH-28) and the maintenance themes (BEH-70, theory only) are the closest evidence.
- No evidence was found on the best "minimum viable session" dose for keeping adherence. The fallback-goal rule is an expert recommendation plus an extrapolation from the dropout predictors.
- No study was found on wording for referrals or for declining unsafe requests in fitness coaching. The template is expert opinion built from MI definitions.
- Weight-stigma evidence comes from healthcare and public-health settings, not specifically from fitness coaching.
- Communicating uncertainty: the only experimental evidence used (BEH-57) concerns news facts, not coaching.
