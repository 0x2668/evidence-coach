# END: Endurance (running, cycling, trail and ultra)

Scope: intensity zones and their anchors, training intensity distribution, interval versus continuous training, running volume progression and injury, long runs and trail/ultra preparation, heat preparation, tapering, strength training for endurance, and reading training data for programming decisions. Race fuelling and fluids are NUT; sRPE, ACWR and HRV monitoring are MON; heat illness and hyponatremia emergencies are SAF; general strength-training variables are STR.

## Practical rules for the agent

### Intensity zones: one reference model
- Use the 3-zone model as the internal reference for every plan: zone 1 below the first threshold (LT1/VT1, blood lactate about 2 mM or less), zone 2 between the first and second threshold (about 2-4 mM, up to LT2/VT2 or critical speed/power), zone 3 above the second threshold. [END-02, END-13]
- Always say which scheme a zone number belongs to. In the 3-zone research model "zone 2" is the moderate between-threshold zone; in common 5-zone schemes zone 2 is still easy (below LT1). In the plan table, write the 3-zone label plus a plain word (easy, steady/threshold, hard). [END-02, END-03]
- Map 5-zone schemes onto 3 zones as follows: 5-zone Z1-Z2 = 3-zone Z1, Z3 = Z2, Z4-Z5 = Z3. In elite skiers these bands sat at about 54-83% HRmax (Z1-Z2), 84-88% (Z3) and 89% or more (Z4-Z5), but treat %HRmax bands as rough for individuals. [END-03, END-12]
- Anchor zones to thresholds (lab LT1/VT1, critical speed or power, or RPE and talk test), not to fixed percentages of HRmax or VO2max, which place different people in different domains. [END-12, END-13]
- Without any testing (beginners, general fitness): zone 1 = comfortable full-sentence talking, RPE up to about 10-11 on the 6-20 scale; zone 2 = speech starting to become uncomfortable up to RPE about 13-15; zone 3 = comfortable speech not possible, RPE above about 15. The talk test was consistent across walking, jogging, cycling and elliptical exercise but is impractical during high-intensity intervals, where RPE, pace or power should be used instead. [END-14, END-66, END-67]
- Heart-rate max: use a measured value (a recent all-out race finish or maximal test) when available. If not, estimate HRmax as 208 - 0.7 x age and tell the athlete the estimate can be off by about 11 beats/min or more, which can shift a zone; cross-check HR zones with RPE and the talk test for the first 2-3 weeks. In a sample of 99 adults tested on a treadmill, all nine age-based equations agreed poorly with measured HRmax for individuals, so a measured maximal effort beats any formula [END-15, END-16, END-14, END-60, END-61].
- If only heart-rate anchors exist, prescribe with %heart-rate reserve (Karvonen: resting HR + % x (HRmax - resting HR)) rather than %HRmax. [END-17]

### Running pace zones from race results or a field test
- Estimate critical speed (CS) from at least three maximal efforts of different length lasting roughly 3-15 min (for example 1500 m, 3 km, 5 km races or time trials). Do not use 800 m or shorter, or 10 km or longer. CS is about 95-99% of the speed of the longest effort. Keep the same protocol for re-tests. [END-19, END-20]
- Set the top of easy running (zone 1) at about 82% of CS (about 81% for slower runners, 83-84% for faster runners); zone 2 runs from there up to CS; zone 3 is faster than CS. Confirm with RPE and the talk test. [END-21, END-14]
- CS can only be held for about 20-30 min: do not prescribe continuous efforts at CS longer than that; "threshold" or tempo runs sit just below CS. [END-18]
- Marathon pacing guide: recreational runners race the marathon at roughly 79% (360-minute finishers) to 93% (150-minute finishers) of CS. Flag a planned first half above 94% of CS as high risk of a large second-half slowdown. CS can also be estimated from the best 400 m to 5 km efforts in GPS training data. [END-22]

### Cycling power zones and FTP
- Treat FTP (95% of a 20-min all-out test) as a repeatable performance marker for zone setting and tracking, not as an exact threshold: individual error is about plus or minus 9% (up to about 40-60 W), and riders held FTP for about 51 min on average, not 60 (cyclists in another trial failed after about 33.7 min at FTP). [END-23, END-25, END-26, END-62]
- For sustained threshold work of 20 min or longer, expect the true steady-state limit to be below FTP for many riders (in one study MLSS was about 93% of FTP). Cap long threshold intervals by RPE (no higher than about 13-15) and by heart rate stabilising rather than drifting up; lower the power target if it does not. [END-24, END-14, END-49]
- After a training block, re-test with the same protocol; an unchanged FTP does not prove that the threshold has not moved. Do not describe FTP as the boundary between heavy and severe intensity [END-24, END-63].
- Cycling periodization: no block, traditional or day-by-day model is better supported over 8 to 12 weeks in trained road cyclists; reported volumes in those studies were about 8.75 to 11.68 hours per week in block studies. Use these as descriptive reference only [END-64, END-65].

### Training intensity distribution
- Make most training easy. Competitive endurance athletes do about 80% of sessions at low intensity and about 20% hard (about 4 easy sessions per hard session); measured by time, elite athletes spend about 84-95% in zone 1, 2-11% in zone 2 and 2-9% in zone 3. [END-01, END-04]
- For recreational athletes default to a pyramidal distribution (mostly zone 1, some zone 2, a little zone 3). Polarized training is not clearly better for them; for time-limited recreational runners a plan with more zone 2 gave similar results in less time. [END-07, END-08, END-11, END-10]
- For competitive, highly trained athletes, a polarized block shorter than 12 weeks may raise VO2max slightly more; time-trial performance does not differ between distributions. Use polarized phases as an option, not a requirement. [END-06, END-08, END-07]

### Time-limited and general-fitness clients (including the sedentary 45-year-old)
- Both continuous training and high-intensity intervals raise VO2max substantially (about 5 ml/kg/min versus no exercise in healthy young to middle-aged adults); intervals add about 1 ml/kg/min on average, including in middle-aged and older adults. Offer intervals when time is short, after the client tolerates regular easy sessions. [END-27, END-28, END-29]
- Busy weeks and maintenance: endurance performance was maintained for up to 15 weeks with as few as 2 sessions per week, or with 33-66% less volume (as low as 13-26 minutes per session), if intensity was kept [END-57].
- For a previously sedentary adult, start with easy sessions guided by the talk test and RPE; if running, start with run-walk: three sessions per week at a conversational pace, for example 10 x (1 min run, 1 min walk) in week 1, building gradually to continuous running. Pre-exercise screening belongs to SAF. [END-14, END-31]

### Running volume progression and injury risk
- Beginners carry about twice the injury rate per hour of running of recreational runners (about 18 versus 8 injuries per 1000 h), so progress them most conservatively. [END-35] Across prospective studies the injury incidence was 14.9% in novice, 26.1% in recreational and 62.6% in competitive runners (very wide ranges), most often at the knee (25.8%), foot/ankle (24.4%) and lower leg (24.4%) [END-59].
- Do not present the weekly "10% rule" as protective: a 10%-rule programme did not reduce injuries in novices, and weekly-ratio spikes were not associated with injury in a large cohort. [END-30, END-33]
- Keep every single run, especially the long run, within 10% of the longest run completed in the previous 30 days (the next run may be at most 10% longer than that longest run). Larger single-run spikes raised the overuse-injury rate by about 52% to 128%, depending on spike size. [END-34, END-33]
- For novices, also keep weekly distance increases well below 30% over 2 weeks. [END-32] A systematic review found the links between weekly distance, duration, frequency, intensity or recent changes and injury to be conflicting, so state progression rules as cautious practice, not as proven protection [END-58].

### Trail and ultra preparation (for example a 50 km ultra in 16 weeks)
- Volume reference: experienced adult ultrarunners average about 66-83 km per week. For marathons, more runs per week, more weekly volume, more runs of 32 km or longer and a longer longest run are all associated with faster times. Build frequency first, then volume, then long-run length, within the single-run 10% rule. These are associations, not minimum doses. [END-37, END-36, END-34] Previous ultra experience and marathon time predicted ultra performance most strongly, and ultra runners train with more volume but at slower running speed than marathoners [END-68, END-69].
- Train mainly in zone 1: in a 65-km mountain ultra amateur runners averaged about 77% HRmax and spent about 86% of race time below VT1. Practise long runs and race-specific terrain at that intensity. [END-40]
- Downhill conditioning: introduce downhill running early with short exposures (for example five sessions of 5-15 min at about -10% grade in one week) and repeat it throughout the build, because the protective effect can disappear after about 9 weeks without eccentric loading. After a hard downhill session, allow up to 4-5 days before a key workout or race. [END-41, END-44, END-42, END-43]
- Uphill: on steep climbs, energy cost per metre is more than 5 times the flat cost, and at very steep grades walking costs less than running. Teach power-hiking for steep sections and judge climbing effort by HR and RPE, not pace. [END-46]
- Back-to-back long runs are common but untested practice (practitioner level only). If used before a first 50 km, do them about twice, about 48-64 km over two days, keep each run within the 10% single-run rule, and follow with at least two easy days. [END-45, END-34]
- Expect injuries: in longer ultra races about 50-60% of participants report musculoskeletal problems, mostly ankle and knee [END-70]. Nearly two thirds (64.6%) of ultrarunners lose training days to injury each year; younger and less experienced runners are injured more, and stress fractures (5.5% per year, mostly foot) are more common in women. [END-39]
- Tell ultra athletes that repeated ultra-endurance running may carry long-term cardiovascular (right ventricular dysfunction, arrhythmia, hypertension) and kidney risks; the evidence is low quality. [END-38]
- Hot race: plan heat acclimatisation of at least 60 min per day in the heat for about 1-2 weeks (main adaptations after about 1 week, full benefit after about 2 weeks); benefits are largely kept for 2-4 weeks, so the heat block can finish up to a few weeks before the race. [END-47, END-48]

### Reading Strava, Garmin, FIT and GPX data for programming
- Expect heart rate to drift upward from about 10 min into long steady sessions, more in heat; the drift means relative intensity really rises. For easy and long sessions cap by HR or RPE and accept a slower pace; do not read a hot-day pace drop as lost fitness. [END-49]
- Do not compare raw pace across trail sessions: running cost per metre ranges from about half the flat cost on moderate descents to more than 5 times on steep climbs. Use HR, RPE or grade-adjusted metrics for trail sessions. [END-46]
- Each week, compare the longest run with the longest run in the previous 30 days and flag any run more than 10% longer. [END-34, END-33]
- Use the best 400 m to 5 km efforts in recent data, or recent races of about 3-15 min, to update critical speed and pace zones. [END-22, END-19, END-20]

### Taper
- Taper for about 2 weeks (up to 3 weeks for a marathon or longer): reduce volume progressively by about 41-60%, keep intensity and session frequency. Place the hardest (overload) block just before the taper. In recreational marathoners a steady 3-week taper was associated with about 2.6% faster times. [END-50, END-51, END-52]

### Strength training for endurance athletes
- Runners: add 2-3 strength sessions per week including heavy loads (at least 80% 1RM) and combined methods (heavy plus explosive or plyometric); expect running economy gains of about 2-8% without unwanted body-composition changes. Plyometrics alone is less clearly effective. [END-53, END-55]
- Schedule strength on a separate day or more than 3 hours after a high-intensity run. [END-54]
- Cyclists: prefer heavy strength training. [END-56]

## Red flags and referral triggers
- Localised bone pain during or after running, especially in the foot, in women, or in young or inexperienced ultrarunners: stop the running progression and refer for medical assessment (possible stress fracture; SAF owns the referral rules). [END-39]
- Palpitations, chest symptoms, unexplained loss of performance or fainting in endurance and ultra athletes: refer to a physician before further training (cardiovascular risks in END-38; SAF owns the symptom list). [END-38]
- Heat illness, hyponatremia and race-day emergencies are handled by SAF; heat acclimatisation planning stays here. [END-47]

## Contested areas

### Polarized versus pyramidal versus threshold distribution
- Position A: polarized training is better. An early meta-analysis of 3 small trials found a moderate time-trial benefit over threshold training, and a 2024 meta-analysis found a small VO2peak advantage. [END-05, END-06]
- Position B: the distributions perform similarly. The VO2peak advantage held only in interventions under 12 weeks and in highly trained athletes, with no time-trial difference; a 2025 individual-participant network meta-analysis found no difference between polarized and pyramidal; a 2026 Bayesian network meta-analysis found no model clearly superior; trials in recreational runners found no significant difference, and a threshold-focused plan matched polarized with 17% less time. [END-06, END-07, END-09, END-10, END-11]
- Why they differ: short, small trials, different ways of counting intensity (time in HR zone versus session goal), and training level as a moderator (competitive athletes may favour polarized, recreational athletes pyramidal). [END-08]
- What the agent should do: keep most training easy in all cases [END-01, END-04]; default to pyramidal for recreational and time-limited athletes; offer polarized blocks of under 12 weeks for competitive athletes [END-06, END-08]; let preference, schedule and recovery decide. Tell the athlete: "The research does not show one distribution is clearly best; the consistent finding is that most of your training should be easy."

### The 10% rule and how to progress running volume
- Position A: limit weekly increases (10% rule). Novices increasing weekly distance by more than 30% over 2 weeks tended to have more distance-related injuries (not statistically significant). [END-32]
- Position B: the weekly 10% rule does not prevent injury: an RCT found the same injury rate (about 20%) with and without it, and in a large cohort of runners weekly-ratio spikes were not associated with injury, while single-run spikes over 10% of the 30-day longest run were. [END-30, END-33, END-34]
- Position C: a systematic review of prospective studies found the evidence linking training parameters or their recent changes to injury conflicting and advised caution when recommending optimal progressions [END-58].
- Why they differ: the studies measure different exposures (weekly totals versus single sessions), in different populations, and all are observational except the novice RCT.
- What the agent should do: apply the single-run rule (each run at most 10% longer than the 30-day longest run) [END-34, END-33] and keep beginners' weekly increases modest (well below 30% over 2 weeks) [END-32]; do not promise that any rule prevents injury [END-30, END-34]. Tell the athlete: "No progression rule guarantees safety; the best current data say avoid sudden jumps in the length of any single run."

### Validity of FTP from a 20-minute test
- Position A: FTP (95% of 20-min power) is a practical estimate of the maximal lactate steady state in trained cyclists (bias about 1.4%, limits of agreement about plus or minus 9%). [END-25]
- Position B: FTP overestimates the steady-state limit for many riders (MLSS about 93% of FTP), it cannot be held for 60 min (about 51 min), it did not track training changes in MLSS, and individual limits of agreement are wide; a scoping review concluded FTP is reliable but not interchangeable with lab thresholds; a lactate and oxygen-uptake study concluded FTP is not a heavy-severe boundary (time to failure at FTP about 33.7 min). [END-23, END-24, END-26, END-62, END-63]
- What the agent should do: use FTP for tracking and approximate zones, set long threshold efforts conservatively and check with HR stability and RPE; recommend lab or critical-power testing when precision matters. Tell the athlete: "FTP is a good repeatable test, but it can be a few percent off your true threshold, so we check the numbers against how the efforts feel and what your heart rate does."

### Estimating HRmax from age
- Position A: age equations such as 208 - 0.7 x age are acceptable estimates for healthy adults [END-15, END-16].
- Position B: in 99 treadmill tests all nine equations agreed poorly for individuals; some differed significantly on average (Nes, Gulati, Astrand, male Fairbarn) and the Fox equation was the least likely to under- or overestimate across individuals in that sample [END-60, END-61].
- What the agent should do: use a measured HRmax when available; otherwise treat any formula as a first guess, tell the athlete it can be off by 10 beats/min or more, and cross-check with RPE and the talk test [END-14, END-16, END-60].

### Back-to-back long runs for ultras
- Position A (practitioner): back-to-back long runs are standard ultra preparation; do them about twice before a 50 km. [END-45]
- Position B: no controlled research tests back-to-back long runs; the only related evidence concerns single-run spikes and injury. [END-34]
- What the agent should do: optional; the practitioner source suggests about twice before a first ultra [END-45], with each run within the single-run 10% rule [END-34] and at least two easy days afterwards [END-45]; skip them for injury-prone athletes. Explain that this is coaching practice, not tested science.

## Gaps and open questions
- No controlled studies of ultra preparation: weekly volume, long-run length, back-to-back runs, vertical gain targets and ultra-specific tapers are expert opinion or extrapolated from marathon data.
- Downhill-conditioning doses come from lab studies, mostly in non-runners; the optimal downhill dose for trail runners is unknown.
- The RPE values for thresholds (END-14) come from an abstract that does not state the scale; the 6-20 Borg scale is implied by the values.
- How well device default %HRmax zones match individual thresholds, and how valid grade-adjusted pace algorithms are, is not established here (device validity belongs to MON).
- FTP evidence is mostly from trained male cyclists; data for women and recreational riders are scarce.
- Cycling periodization and volume: only a small systematic review in trained road cyclists exists; no data for recreational riders or for gran fondo or long-event preparation.
- Beginner run-walk programmes have little injury-prevention evidence beyond the GRONORUN trial.
- Bike fit and cycling overuse injuries were not researched.
- Decision for the orchestrator: the knowledge base should fix one zone vocabulary (3-zone model with plain-word labels) so that "zone 2" is never ambiguous between the research model and consumer 5-zone schemes.
